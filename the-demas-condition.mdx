Let me begin where I spend my working life.

Not in a hospital in general — but in the specific room God called me to stand in. I care for mothers. High-risk mothers, and the children they carry. And here is something I have learned in that work that I cannot un-learn:

A woman can feel completely well while something is quietly going wrong inside her that she cannot feel at all.

A mother came to me at twenty-eight weeks. Good color. No pain. She laughed when I asked how she was feeling. *Better than ever*, she said. She had every reason to believe the pregnancy was going beautifully.

But we don't go only by how a patient feels. We measure. We put the probe on the abdomen and we look at what the eye cannot see.

The baby had stopped growing — not recently, but weeks before. Severely. The umbilical blood flow had become dangerously compromised. The placenta had been quietly failing. And this mother, carrying that child every moment of every day, had felt none of it.

She was not in pain. She was in danger.

The only reason she is alive today, and her child is alive today, is that someone looked beneath the surface at what could not be felt.

---

I didn't come here to talk to you about a baby's growth chart. I came to talk about the other kind of life. The inner one. The hidden one.

Because a Christian can do the very same thing.

A Christian can pray, serve, give, teach, lead — can carry the whole outward shape of a faithful life — and be quietly losing the heart the entire time.

That is not a hypothetical. That is a man in the Bible. His name was Demas.

---

## Two Verses, One Autopsy

The whole case is contained in two short lines, written years apart, by the same hand. Paul's hand.

The first is so easy to read past. It sits at the very end of Colossians, in the closing greetings — the part most of us skim.

> *"Luke the beloved physician, and Demas, greet you."*
> — Colossians 4:14, NKJV

Look at where Demas is standing in that sentence. He is next to Luke. He is inside Paul's inner circle, active in apostolic ministry, named in Scripture beside one of the most faithful men in the New Testament. By every visible measure, Demas is fine.

That is the first scan. Everything looks normal.

Now, same author, several years later. Paul is in his final imprisonment, near the end of his life. He writes a sentence that should stop every one of us cold:

> *"Demas has forsaken me, having loved this present world."*
> — 2 Timothy 4:10, NKJV

That is the second scan. And the diagnosis is terminal.

Notice what Paul does *not* say. He does not say Demas was caught in scandal. He doesn't say Demas denied the faith publicly, or fell into some dramatic visible sin. There is no explosion in this story.

The explanation is quieter than that. And because it is quieter, it is more frightening.

*He loved this present world.*

Two verses, side by side — a complete spiritual autopsy. The first shows you a man who looks alive. The second tells you when he died. And the cause of death was not rebellion.

The cause of death was misdirected love.

---

## He Did Not Fall. He Drifted.

Here is what arrests me as a physician.

Demas did not collapse all at once. Nobody does. In medicine, the dramatic event — the crisis in the delivery room, the heart that stops — is almost never the beginning of the disease. It is the *end* of it. The crisis is the moment the body finally announces what has been developing silently, in the dark, for a long time.

That is Demas.

Somewhere between Paul's first Roman imprisonment, around AD 60–62, and his final imprisonment around AD 67, Demas made a series of small choices. Not one great betrayal. A thousand small redirections. A slow turning of the inner compass, a few degrees at a time, until his whole course pointed somewhere it was never meant to go.

He did not fall. He drifted.

And that is exactly why this should sober us — because drifting does not feel like sin. Drifting feels like *nothing*. The current does the work. You don't have to swim away from God to end up far from Him. You only have to stop swimming toward Him.

One Greek word matters here. When Paul says Demas *loved* this present world, the word is *ēgapēsen* — from *agapaō*. That is not a weak word. It is the love word. The high one. The same word Scripture uses for the love of God Himself. Demas gave his *allegiance*, his inner devotion, the weight of his affection, to something that was never going to last.

And the word for "world" is not the planet. It's *aiōn* — the present age. The spirit of the times. The mood of the moment. Demas fell in love with what the era could offer.

So hear me carefully, because this is the center of the diagnosis. The great conflict of the Christian life is not mainly a conflict of ideas. It is a conflict of *affections*. The enemy of your soul rarely walks up to a mature believer and asks them to deny Christ. He is more patient than that.

He only needs to train you — slowly, gently, over years — to love something else more.

That is how a praying, serving, giving believer dies on the inside while the outside keeps functioning.

Blood flow choked off, a little at a time, while the patient still feels fine.

## Two Men. One Gospel. Two Endings.

I need you to go back to that first verse, because I left something there on purpose.

*"Luke the beloved physician, and Demas, greet you."*

Two names. Standing side by side. In that sentence, you cannot tell them apart. Same circle. Same apostle. Same gospel. Same access, same opportunity. If you had visited Paul in those years and met them both, you could not have told which one would finish and which one would fall.

They looked identical on the chart.

But watch what happens by the end. In the very same letter where Paul grieves over Demas, four of the most beautiful words in the New Testament appear:

> *"Only Luke is with me."*
> — 2 Timothy 4:11, NKJV

When others scattered, the beloved physician stayed. When the mission became costly — when there was nothing left to gain and a great deal to lose — Luke was still there.

What made the difference? It wasn't talent. It wasn't nearness to the work. They were equally near.

Write this in your heart: **proximity to the things of God is not the same as surrender to the God of those things.**

Luke was a physician, just as I am. He had a skill set, a reputation, a way to make his own name in the world. He could have leveraged his gifts as a ladder — as so many gifted people do. He didn't. He took those same gifts and laid them on an altar. He bent everything he was good at toward the kingdom of God.

The question the two of them put to every person reading this is not whether you have gifts. It is what your gifts are *for*.

Demas climbed. Luke knelt.

## One Text for the Deepest Question

Before I get to where this lands personally, I want to pause on the verse that has become the hinge of this sermon for me. Not 2 Timothy 4:10. Not the Demas verse.

This one:

> *"Search me, O God, and know my heart; try me, and know my anxious thoughts; and see if there is any grievous way in me, and lead me in the way everlasting."*
> — Psalm 139:23–24, ESV

David calls this a prayer. I call it a diagnostic request.

<Callout type="study" title="Word Study: Psalm 139:23–24">

**חָקַר** (*ḥāqar* — "search me") — to dig, to probe, to investigate thoroughly. This is not a casual glance. The same root is used in Job 29:16 where Job says he "investigated" (*ḥāqar*) cases he did not know. In Proverbs 18:17, it describes thorough cross-examination in a legal dispute. David is not asking God for a general impression. He is asking for the complete workup. The full imaging. Every measurement.

**בָּחַן** (*bāḥan* — "try me / test me") — to assay, to examine as a refiner examines metal. This is the word used for testing the purity of gold. It's the same word in Proverbs 17:3: *"The refining pot is for silver and the furnace for gold, and the LORD tests hearts."* David is asking God to run the metallurgical exam — not just to read the surface but to apply heat and see what comes out.

**שַׂרְעַפַּי** (*śar'appay* — "my anxious thoughts") — this is a fascinating and rare word. It appears only twice in the Hebrew Bible, here and in Psalm 94:19. It carries the sense of *branching* or *divided* thoughts — the inner life that forks in different directions, that hedges, that loves two things at once and calls it faithfulness. David is asking God not just to see his overt rebellion but his *divided* inner life. His *almost-committed* heart. His hedged affections.

**דֶּרֶךְ עוֹלָם** (*derek 'ōlam* — "the way everlasting") — a path that endures beyond the present age. This is the counter to Paul's *aiōn* — the present age that Demas loved. David asks to be led in the *way everlasting*: the road that does not dead-end, the trajectory that outlasts everything this age can offer.

</Callout>

Here is what stops me every time I read this.

David did not pray this prayer from a position of crisis. Psalm 139 is not a psalm written from prison or from exile or from the immediate aftermath of some public collapse. It is a meditation on divine omniscience — a sustained, almost overwhelmed reflection on the fact that God already knows everything. He knows where you sit and where you rise (v. 2). He knows your words before you speak them (v. 4). There is nowhere you can go from His presence (v. 7–12).

And David's response to the God who already knows everything is to *ask* to be known.

Not to confess. Not to perform. Not to explain himself. To *request the examination*.

That is the bravest prayer in the Bible. Because most of us spend enormous energy curating what God sees — staying on the well-lit side of our inner life, bringing our prayers the pieces we've already resolved, keeping the unsettled parts quiet. We ask God to bless what we've already decided. We rarely ask Him to audit what we think we've settled.

David did the opposite. He invited the full workup.

*Search me. Try me. Show me the branching — the divided loves — I haven't been honest with myself about. And then lead me. Lead me in the way that does not end with this age.*

That is the prayer that catches the disease while it is still treatable.

## What Has Held Me Steady

Let me get personal, because I cannot preach this from a safe distance.

I came to this country when I was four years old. My parents brought me from Nigeria to a place where the winter was a kind of argument that everything familiar was now far away. New language. New food. New rhythms. And from that day to this one, my life has been a long series of transitions — new schools, new cities, training that cost more than I knew how to pay at the time, a profession that is exacting and generous in equal measure.

Every one of those transitions came with the same whisper I now recognize as the voice of *aiōn* — the present age: *This is what matters now. This is the prize. Build this. Love this. Become this.*

I want to be honest in a way that is not entirely comfortable for me.

There have been seasons in my life when the practice was on the throne. When the recognition felt essential rather than incidental. When the next credential, the next milestone, was not just what I was working toward — it was what I was quietly living *for*. Not consciously. Not with my words.

But with the actual weight of my attention.

And I was still in church. Still on the elder board. Still serving. Still praying.

The outside looked fine. The inside was drifting.

The only thing that has caught me — every time — is not willpower. It is not discipline. It is not the absence of ambition. The only thing that has caught me is what Paul names in the text that Demas lost: a love that is *greater* than the love of this present age.

Everything in my life has been temporary except the One who does not change.

That — that — is what Demas let go of. And what Luke held onto, all the way to the end.

## Laodicea Is Not Ancient History

Now I have to stop talking about Demas and start talking about us.

Seventh-day Adventists have been given the very language for what Paul is describing. We call it the Great Controversy. We understand that the conflict between Christ and Satan is not primarily a conflict of doctrines — it is a contest over the human heart. And we know, from the Adventist reading of Revelation, how that contest ends.

We know about Laodicea.

In Revelation 3:17, Jesus speaks to a church that sounds remarkably like Demas. Not openly hostile. Not apostate. Not denying the faith. A church that says, *I am rich and increased with goods and have need of nothing.* And Christ — not a critic, but Christ Himself — looks at that church and says: *you do not know that you are wretched, miserable, poor, blind, and naked.*

That is the Demas condition wearing ecclesiastical clothing.

And the Adventist pioneers understood this was not only a first-century portrait. It is a portrait of the end-time church. It is, in our reading, a portrait of *us*. Ellen White did not soften this: *"Unless we do yield ourselves to the control of Christ, we shall be dominated by the wicked one."* (*The Desire of Ages*, p. 324)

There is no neutral ground.

We can lead ministries. We can teach the Sabbath School lesson. We can sit on the board. We can tithe faithfully. We can do all of it — and keep the whole outward architecture of faith standing while the inner life goes cold toward the actual presence of God.

**You can lose your heart in the third pew, with your hand raised.**

The Laodicean condition is not diagnosed from the outside. It feels like having need of nothing. It feels like being fine.

Feeling fine is not a testimony. It may be a symptom.

## Check Your Vitals

The good news is that Jesus does not merely expose the disease. He holds the cure.

To the very church He diagnoses — to Laodicea, to us — He still says: *buy from Me gold refined in the fire, white garments to cover you, and eye-salve that you may see.* Refined faith, tested in the fire. His righteousness, covering what we cannot fix. And sight — honest sight — to see ourselves at last.

He is not standing over the chart shaking His head. He is holding the remedy. He has been holding it the whole time.

So before you close this page, I want to ask you to do the thing I do every week for patients who feel perfectly well. I want to ask you to check your vitals.

Not your blood pressure. The other one.

Ask the Holy Spirit the hard question, and then be quiet long enough to hear the answer. *What do I love most? What occupies the center of my imagination when no one is watching? What would I struggle to surrender — even now — if Christ asked me for it tonight?*

If something just came to mind — don't run from it. That is not condemnation. That is the eye-salve working. That is the scan finding the thing while there is still time to treat it. That is grace, arriving early.

Do not wait for a public collapse to seek a private cure.

The Demas condition is real. It is quiet. It is patient. And it is treatable — but only after an honest diagnosis.

Pray the brave prayer. Open the whole chart. Let the Great Physician do what He alone can do.

Check your vitals.

The Physician is in.
