I want to ask you something before you read another word.

When was the last time you let holiness actually land on you?

Not the idea of holiness. Not the theology of holiness, filed away in a sermon outline. The actual weight of it — the kind that makes a trained prophet fall apart at the seams and cry out that he is ruined.

Isaiah 6 is one of the most familiar passages in Scripture. Preachers open with it. Seminary students memorize it. It gets preached at ordinations and installations because it has all the right ingredients: the throne, the seraphim, the coal, the call.

But Stephane Beaulieu, writing in the June 2026 issue of *Ministry Magazine*, makes an argument that deserves more attention than it has gotten. Isaiah 6, he says, is not primarily a call narrative. It is a judgment scene. The call is real — but it comes *after* the verdict, *through* the cleansing, and only as a consequence of what God does to Isaiah on the courtroom floor of heaven.

That changes everything about what it means to say, "Here am I. Send me."

---

## The King Has Not Left the Throne

The vision opens with a simple historical note: King Uzziah has died.

This is not a throwaway detail. Uzziah reigned for fifty-two years. He was the political certainty of an entire generation. His death would have felt to Judah something like the ground shifting beneath your feet — not dramatically, not suddenly, but enough to make you reach for a wall.

Into that moment of instability, Isaiah is given a vision:

<div class="scripture">
  "In the year that King Uzziah died, I saw the Lord, high and exalted, seated on a throne; and the train of his robe filled the temple."
  <cite>Isaiah 6:1</cite>
</div>

The Hebrew word translated "seated" carries the meaning not only of sitting but of *dwelling* — administering, presiding, inhabiting. God is not an emergency substitute for Uzziah. He is the permanent occupant. He is where He has always been. Doing what He has always done.

I find that I need this more than I want to admit.

Medicine trains you to manage instability. When someone crashes in a delivery room, you learn not to let your face show what your chest is doing. You hold the room together. You be the ceiling. It is a useful skill and a dangerous habit — because you can spend so many years being the ceiling for others that you forget you also need one.

The throne in Isaiah 6 is my ceiling.

When the thing I trusted — the institution, the stability, the era — dies, the Lord is still seated. Still administering. Still there.

<div class="callout callout-insight">
  <strong>The Permanence of the Throne</strong><br />
  Uzziah's fifty-two-year reign defined a generation. When it ended, the political ground shifted. Isaiah's vision answers the moment directly: the Lord is not a regent filling the vacancy. He is the permanent occupant of heaven's throne — presiding, dwelling, administering. "Seated" (*yashav*) means He never left.
</div>

## The Robe and the Altar

Beaulieu's most important exegetical move is this: the Lord in Isaiah's vision is not just king. He is simultaneously high priest.

<div class="scripture">
  "Above him were seraphim, each with six wings: With two wings they covered their faces, with two they covered their feet, and with two they were flying. And they were calling to one another: 'Holy, holy, holy is the LORD Almighty; the whole earth is full of his glory.'"
  <cite>Isaiah 6:2–3</cite>
</div>

The hem of His robe filling the temple is not royal imagery. In the Hebrew Bible, the term *hem* specifically refers to the high priest's robe (Exodus 28:33–35). And the word *hekal* — temple — carries both meanings in Hebrew: palace *and* temple. The One on the throne presides over both chambers simultaneously.

This matters because king and high priest are not the same office. A king adjudicates. A high priest intercedes. A king issues verdicts. A high priest provides atonement. The fact that Isaiah sees both offices held by one Person — the same Person — is the theological hinge on which the entire passage turns.

Beaulieu connects the smoke filling the temple to Leviticus 16: the Day of Atonement. When Aaron entered the Most Holy Place, he took a censer of burning coals and sweet incense to create a protective cloud of smoke. Not because God needed ambiance. Because the smoke served as a veiling mechanism — it covered the priest in the presence of perfect holiness so he would not die.

The smoke in Isaiah 6 is doing the same work. It is not atmosphere. It is mercy.

<div class="callout callout-theology">
  <strong>King and High Priest — Simultaneously</strong><br />
  The Hebrew <em>hekal</em> means both palace and temple. The hem of the Lord's robe is priestly garment language (Exodus 28:33–35). The smoke echoes the incense cloud of Leviticus 16. Beaulieu's point: Isaiah does not see only a throne room. He sees a throne room that is also a sanctuary. The One presiding is both Judge and Intercessor — which means no verdict is delivered without atonement being immediately available.
</div>

## The Diagnostic Moment

Then Isaiah hears himself.

<div class="scripture">
  "'Woe to me!' I cried. 'I am ruined! For I am a man of unclean lips, and I live among a people of unclean lips, and my eyes have seen the King, the LORD Almighty.'"
  <cite>Isaiah 6:5</cite>
</div>

Most English translations render the Hebrew *damah* as "lost" or "ruined." But Beaulieu notes that the word also carries the meaning of "cut off" — the same consequence attached to those who refused to humble themselves on the Day of Atonement (Leviticus 23:29).

Isaiah does not cry out because he has suddenly discovered that he is imperfect. He cries out because standing in the presence of perfect holiness, surrounded by seraphim who cover their faces and cry *holy, holy, holy*, he receives the clearest diagnostic reading of his life. The contrast is total. The gap is visible. He is unclean. His people are unclean. And he has been speaking — with unclean lips — into the public record of a nation under covenant with the Holy One.

I have been in rooms where a diagnosis came in that no one in the family was ready for. There is a particular silence that follows. It is not the silence of peace. It is the silence of a world rearranging itself around a fact that cannot be unfact-ed.

Isaiah's "woe is me" is that silence.

He is not performing grief. He is receiving a result.

## Heaven Does Not Just Report the Pathology

This is the turn I do not want to rush through.

<div class="scripture">
  "Then one of the seraphim flew to me with a live coal in his hand, which he had taken with tongs from the altar. With it he touched my mouth and said, 'See, this has touched your lips; your guilt is taken away and your sin atoned for.'"
  <cite>Isaiah 6:6–7</cite>
</div>

A seraph flies to Isaiah. It carries a coal taken from the altar — almost certainly the altar of incense, located just before the veil of the Most Holy Place. The same altar whose smoke ascends before God. The same altar where atonement meets intercession.

The coal touches Isaiah's mouth.

*Your iniquity is taken away. Your sin is atoned for.*

Beaulieu's language here is precise: the two terms — *iniquity* and *sin* — are both sanctuary vocabulary. *Iniquity* (*avon*) describes Cain's punishment, his outcry against the consequences of what he had done. *Sin* (*chata'ah*) is a term woven into the fabric of the purification offerings — it is what the sanctuary absorbs on behalf of the repentant offerer.

What the seraph carries from the altar is not destruction. It is the means of cleansing.

God does not look at Isaiah and say: *I see what you are. Disqualified.*

God looks at Isaiah and says: *I see what you are. Let Me touch the wound.*

I am an MFM physician. I have spent my career working at the edge of what human bodies can survive. High-risk pregnancies. Anomalies. Maternal conditions that could be lethal without precise intervention. And I have learned that the most dangerous moment in medicine is not when the diagnosis is bad. The most dangerous moment is when someone receives a bad diagnosis and no one tells them what the treatment looks like.

Isaiah receives the diagnosis. He also receives the treatment.

<div class="callout callout-pastoral">
  <strong>The Order Matters</strong><br />
  Isaiah 6 is often preached as a call narrative. But read the verse numbers in sequence: the encounter comes first (vv. 1–4), the diagnosis second (v. 5), the cleansing third (vv. 6–7), and only then the commission (v. 8). The call comes last — not because it is least important, but because it cannot precede the coal. This is the pastoral heart of Isaiah 6 that tends to get lost in ordination sermons.
</div>

## The Adventist Reading

I want to name this explicitly, because it matters.

Seventh-day Adventist theology has a judgment problem — not a theological problem, but a pastoral one. We have sometimes preached the investigative judgment in ways that made the courtroom feel like a hostile environment, as though God were looking for reasons to condemn rather than reasons to restore. The effect on ordinary believers has not always been assurance. It has sometimes been chronic anxiety about whether they have confessed enough, repented deeply enough, been good enough for long enough.

Isaiah 6 is not that judgment.

The scene Beaulieu describes is Daniel 7 before Daniel uses the word Daniel: the Ancient of Days, clothed in white, on a flaming throne, with thousands ministering to him. It is Leviticus 16 rendered in prophetic vision. It is the Day of Atonement seen from inside the sanctuary.

And the operative question is not: *Will Isaiah survive the verdict?*

The operative question is: *What does God do when a repentant, honest sinner stands before perfect holiness?*

The answer is the coal.

<div class="callout callout-theology">
  <strong>The Investigative Judgment and the Coal</strong><br />
  SDA theology has sometimes preached the judgment as a search for failures. Isaiah 6 corrects that framing. The investigation of the sanctuary is not God looking for reasons to condemn — it is God providing what is needed: cleansing, restoration, commissioning. The operative question of Isaiah 6 is not whether Isaiah will survive the verdict. It is what God does when a repentant sinner stands before perfect holiness. The coal is the answer.
</div>

God is not content to leave Isaiah in his condition. He is not content to leave us in ours.

The sanctuary is open. The High Priest is interceding. The coal is still in His hand.

## The Interior Question

Before I close, I want to stay here for a moment.

Because Isaiah 6 implicates me in a specific way.

I am an elder at Atlanta North Seventh-day Adventist Church. I preach. I teach Sabbath School. I write. I have opinions about theology and medicine and the direction of the church and the nation. I diagnose — systems, situations, other people's blind spots.

And sometimes I move through all of that without stopping long enough to say: *Woe is me.*

Beaulieu asks whether Isaiah, standing before God in the temple, was functioning in that moment as a priest — intervening on behalf of the people. If so, the coal that touched his lips was not only personal cleansing. It was preparation for priestly intercession.

Which means the question is not just: *Have I been cleansed?*

The question is: *Am I bringing unclean lips to the work of intercession — the preaching, the teaching, the writing — without first standing in the smoke and letting the coal do its work?*

I do not ask this as performance. I ask it because I feel the weight of it.

## The Call Comes Last

<div class="scripture">
  "Then I heard the voice of the Lord saying, 'Whom shall I send? And who will go for us?' And I said, 'Here am I. Send me!'"
  <cite>Isaiah 6:8</cite>
</div>

Preachers love to preach that line. I love to preach that line. It is the right response. It is the posture of availability, the surrender of personal agenda to divine purpose.

But we have to read the verse numbers in order.

Verse 8 comes after verse 7. The call comes after the coal. The commission follows the cleansing.

God does not send unclean lips to do holy work and call it sacrifice. He provides cleansing first. Always. The transformation precedes the commission.

Whatever God is calling you to — whatever is waiting on the other side of "here am I" — the question worth sitting with is this: Have you let Him touch the wound first?

The throne is occupied. The High Priest is interceding. The smoke is still rising.

The coal before the call.
