A sermon outline can be generated in seconds. A Bible passage can become a social media post before we have finished our morning coffee. We can move from reading a verse to explaining it publicly with almost no interruption.

Where, in that process, does the verse get to change us?

That question sits at the center of Ezra 7:10. In his November 2026 *Ministry* editorial, [“The three imperatives of spiritual leadership: Seek, do, teach,”](https://www.ministrymagazine.org/archive/2026/11/26-11-editorial) Jongimpi D. Papu draws attention to the pattern within this verse: Ezra sought God's Word, practiced it, and taught it. Papu's concern is the formation of the person entrusted with spiritual leadership.

I want to sit with the passage itself and the invitation it extends to everyone who influences another person's faith.

Call it **the ministry of 7:10**: a life in which the heart is prepared, Scripture is sought, obedience is practiced, and truth is shared.

<div class="scripture">
  "For Ezra had prepared his heart to seek the law of the LORD, and to do it, and to teach in Israel statutes and judgments."
  <cite>Ezra 7:10, KJV</cite>
</div>

## The Heart Before the Assignment

Ezra enters the story as a priest and a scribe skilled in the law of Moses (Ezra 7:6). He belongs to a people rebuilding their communal life after exile, under Persian rule. Their task includes worship, identity, justice, and the recovery of covenant faithfulness. They need someone who can help them understand what belonging to God means in practice.

The chapter gives Ezra credentials. Verse 10 lets us see his intention.

He had “prepared his heart.” In biblical language, the heart includes thought, desire, and decision. Ezra had directed his inner life toward a purpose. His competence had a spiritual center.

We prepare many things for ministry. We prepare slides, schedules, discussion questions, microphones, and the closing appeal. Each has its place. This verse asks us to include the person who will stand behind all that preparation.

What am I bringing to the text? A willingness to be corrected? A grievance I hope Scripture will help me prosecute? A desire for recognition? Weariness that I have never brought honestly to God?

Preparing the heart begins with making room for an answer we did not arrange in advance.

## Seek: Give the Text Your Attention

Ezra committed himself to seeking the law of the Lord. In its setting, this means sustained attention to God's instruction given to Israel. Christian readers receive that testimony within the whole biblical story, which finds its center in Jesus Christ.

Seeking requires patience. Read the paragraph around the verse. Notice who is speaking, who is listening, and what problem the passage addresses. Ask how the text contributes to Scripture's account of God's character and His dealings with people.

Those habits matter in an attention economy that rewards immediate reaction. A screenshot can circulate widely while leaving the argument surrounding it unread. A familiar phrase can feel so settled that we stop listening to the passage in which it appears.

For Seventh-day Adventists, who have placed Bible study at the center of our identity, this is a particular responsibility. Our Sabbath School classes, evangelistic teaching, and family worship depend on careful reading. The confidence to open a Bible should travel with the humility to discover something we have overlooked.

There is a useful parallel in medicine. Knowing a guideline includes understanding when it applies, what evidence supports it, and how it bears on the patient before us. Responsible care requires attention. Responsible biblical interpretation does too.

**Give the text enough time to ask something of you.**

## Do: Let Understanding Become a Decision

The next movement in Ezra 7:10 brings Scripture into conduct: “and to do it.”

James makes the same demand explicit:

<div class="scripture">
  "But be ye doers of the word, and not hearers only, deceiving your own selves."
  <cite>James 1:22, KJV</cite>
</div>

Self-deception is a sobering diagnosis. We can mistake the satisfaction of understanding a passage for the surrender it requires. We can leave a compelling Bible study feeling spiritually accomplished while carrying the same resentment home.

Obedience gives understanding a concrete form. A passage about truthful speech reaches the message we are about to send. A command to love our neighbor reaches the person whose needs complicate our schedule. Teaching about forgiveness reaches the conversation we keep postponing. Sabbath, the weekly rest Adventists receive as a gift from God, reaches our willingness to entrust unfinished work to Him.

The question becomes specific: What decision does this text place before me today?

That specificity should be joined to grace. Ephesians 2:8–10 locates salvation in God's gift and describes good works as the life for which He creates us in Christ. Obedience grows within that gift. We bring our resistance, weakness, and failure to the Savior who forgives and renews us.

Ezra's own chapter gives us that setting. Immediately before the statement about his prepared heart, the account credits his safe arrival to “the good hand of his God upon him” (Ezra 7:9). His devotion belongs within a story sustained by divine help.

The ministry of 7:10 is possible because God is at work in the people He calls.

## Teach: Help Someone Else Grow

Ezra's preparation opens outward: he intends “to teach in Israel statutes and judgments.” What he seeks and practices becomes a resource for the community.

Nehemiah 8 offers a vivid picture of this work. Ezra reads the law before the assembled people, and the Levites help them understand it. The account emphasizes the people's comprehension (Nehemiah 8:1–8). Faithful teaching gives people access to the meaning of the Word and helps them respond.

That purpose reaches beyond the pulpit. A parent explaining repentance to a child, a Sabbath School teacher welcoming a difficult question, and a friend opening Scripture with someone in grief all participate in this work.

Jesus gives teaching its living form. In John 13, He washes His disciples' feet and calls them to follow His example. His instruction comes with a basin, a towel, and attention to the people in the room.

Teaching carries that same practical responsibility. Explain carefully. Invite questions. Acknowledge what you still need to study. Help the person before you take a faithful next step.

## A Pattern We Keep Returning To

Ezra 7:10 gives us a searching order for ministry. It also describes commitments we must keep renewing.

We continue to seek while we practice. Teaching exposes questions that send us back to study. An attempt at obedience reveals how deeply we need prayer. Growth keeps these movements in conversation throughout a lifetime.

This matters for anyone who reads the verse and feels immediately disqualified. A Christian can share faithfully while still being formed. Honest confession and a willingness to receive correction belong to credible witness. Claiming spiritual maturity we have never attained damages it.

As a physician, elder, and writer, I feel the personal weight of this passage. Every explanation I offer gives me another opportunity to ask whether I have attended to its claim on my own life. Writing about patience places my speech under examination. Teaching dependence on God brings my own habits of control into view.

The same Word I open for someone else remains open over me.

## Practice 7:10 This Week

Choose a manageable passage and return to it over several days. Begin with prayer for a receptive heart. Then carry three questions into the week:

1. **Seek:** What does this passage say in context, and what does it reveal about God?
2. **Do:** What specific response can I make today, asking for God's help?
3. **Teach:** Who could I help understand this truth, and how can I share it with care?

At the end of the week, return to the passage. Give thanks for any growth. Confess where you resisted. Ask someone you trust to help you see what you have missed.

Perhaps your next act of ministry will begin with an apology. Perhaps it will begin with listening through a question you usually interrupt. Perhaps it will begin with opening the Bible quietly, allowing God time to address the person who is always preparing to address everyone else.

Prepare the heart. Seek the Word. Take the next obedient step. Help someone else walk with Christ.

**Let the next person you teach meet someone the Word is still changing.**
