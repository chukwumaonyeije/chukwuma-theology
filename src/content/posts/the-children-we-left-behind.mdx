*Oliver Twist, public schools, and the Christian responsibility to see the system*

The question that stayed with me after the recent debate about Nikole Hannah-Jones and her daughter’s education was larger than the question everyone seemed to be answering.

Hannah-Jones had chosen public schools for her daughter in New York City as part of her commitment to confronting school segregation. Years later, her daughter, Najya, had serious objections to those choices. Their conversations, featured in *This American Life*, brought the tension into uncomfortable focus: a parent’s convictions about the common good can carry consequences that her child experiences very differently. ([“An Argument,” This American Life](https://www.thisamericanlife.org/898/an-argument))

That deserves careful attention. A child’s account of her own education matters. We should listen without immediately turning her into evidence for whichever position we already hold.

But much of the public argument seemed to narrow around one question: Did this mother sacrifice her child’s educational interests for a social principle?

I left a comment on Spotify:

> “We scrutinize individual choices so intensely that the system escapes judgment. Yes, parents are accountable, but they did not create segregated schools. The greater indictment belongs to a system that forces families to choose between protecting their children and advancing the common good.”

The comment unexpectedly resonated with other listeners.

Perhaps it named something many of us recognize but struggle to articulate. We can examine a parent’s decision closely and still leave the conditions producing that decision almost entirely unexamined.

A mother becomes the defendant. The educational system becomes the scenery.

Yet the scenery is part of the story.

Why should providing an excellent education for one’s own child require escaping the institution educating somebody else’s child?

## The Decision We Made

I cannot approach this question as a detached observer.

In 2003, my family purchased a home north of Atlanta. We would not move into it until October, but our oldest son needed to begin school in August. We enrolled him in the public school available to us during that interval.

He lasted two days.

As newcomers to Atlanta, my wife and I were shocked by the environment and concerned about what we believed the school could provide him. He would have attended for only a few months, but even that felt too long.

We removed him and enrolled him in an Adventist private school.

That decision shaped far more than the next few months. Our children went through Adventist elementary, middle, and high schools. One later attended an Adventist college. Looking back, I believe they benefited substantially from the education they received. I remain grateful for it.

And yet, more than twenty years later, I still remember the children we left behind.

Our son left. The school remained. The other students remained.

Their parents presumably wanted their children to flourish, too. They wanted safety, encouragement, competent instruction, and a future with room to grow. I have no reason to imagine that we loved our son more than they loved their children.

What we possessed was an option.

We could recognize a problem and arrange an alternative. Whatever choices other families had, they were not necessarily the choices available to us.

Once our own child was safe, the immediate problem had been solved.

But it had only been solved for us.

That is why the Hannah-Jones debate unsettled me. My family made a different decision, and I still believe our decision benefited our children. I am also left with a question our decision could not answer.

What happened to everyone else?

## What Do We Owe a Child?

A parent has an extraordinary obligation toward his or her own child.

Children should not become instruments of their parents’ political, social, or theological projects. Their needs deserve attention in their own right. Their objections deserve a hearing. A worthy public commitment does not automatically make every private consequence acceptable.

“What do I owe my child?” is therefore a legitimate and unavoidable question.

But another question sits beside it: “What do we owe every child?”

Answering the first does not dispose of the second.

Imagine many families independently making the decision we made. Each parent may act prudently. Each may have compelling reasons to leave. Yet, where departure draws away families with greater resources, time, or influence, the children who remain may face an even more difficult environment.

The individual decision can be understandable while the collective pattern remains morally troubling.

That distinction matters. It allows us to take parental responsibility seriously while examining the arrangements within which parents exercise it.

As a physician, I know that treating the patient in front of me is an immediate responsibility. I also know that recurring injuries should provoke questions about the conditions producing them. When the same harm keeps appearing, attending to each case remains necessary. So does understanding the pattern.

Christian moral reasoning requires that wider attention.

Agency is real. Context is also real.

## Oliver Asks for More

Around the same time, I was reading Charles Dickens’s *Oliver Twist*.

Victorian workhouses and American schools belong to very different worlds. But Dickens helped me recognize a familiar habit of moral attention.

Oliver is hungry. The other boys are hungry. Eventually, he approaches the master with the request that has become one of literature’s most recognizable sentences:

> “Please, sir, I want some more.”

The institution responds with astonishment and punishment. A child’s request for sufficient food becomes evidence that something is wrong with the child. ([*Oliver Twist*, chapter II](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/730/pg730-images.html))

Dickens invites us to notice the displacement.

The adults could ask why the children are hungry. Instead, they concentrate on the boy who has made their hunger impossible to ignore.

Oliver becomes the problem.

Dickens moves the camera back. We see the child, then the officials, then the workhouse and the assumptions governing it. We begin to see how an institution can consider itself orderly and respectable while treating the suffering in its care as an inconvenience.

The analogy has limits. A contemporary school is not a Victorian workhouse. But the question travels: when someone struggles inside an institution, how quickly do we locate the entire explanation in that person?

A student’s behavior requires examination. So does the classroom.

A parent’s choice requires examination. So do the conditions that made the choice necessary.

Move the camera back.

## When Injustice Becomes a Rule

Scripture repeatedly widens the frame of moral responsibility.

The Hebrew prophets address people’s conduct, but they also speak to rulers, judges, merchants, and communities. They examine the ways power is exercised and the arrangements through which vulnerable people lose protection.

Isaiah 10 begins:

> “Woe unto them that decree unrighteous decrees…”

The next verse identifies the consequences: the poor are deprived of justice, and widows and fatherless children become prey. The condemnation reaches those who write and administer the rules. Injustice has acquired an official form. ([Isaiah 10:1–2, KJV](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Isaiah%2010%3A1-2&version=KJV))

Sometimes injustice looks like a cruel person. Sometimes it looks like a rule, a boundary, a budget, or a procedure that has become so familiar that nobody thinks to question it.

A policy does not become morally innocent because it is familiar, legally authorized, or administered politely. When its predictable effects deprive vulnerable children of ordinary opportunities, Christians must examine both the harm and the decisions that sustain it. “That is how the system works” is a summons to moral scrutiny.

We must ask what policies do, whom they protect, and whose needs they repeatedly leave unmet.

Saying “the system” should begin an investigation. It should lead us toward identifiable decisions and accountable institutions. Otherwise, the phrase can become as evasive as blaming everything on individuals.

The existence of structural injustice does not eliminate personal responsibility. Personal responsibility does not eliminate structural injustice.

Parents make decisions. Teachers, administrators, legislators, and communities make decisions. Those choices occur within conditions that none of them created alone, but that many of them have some capacity to influence.

Christian theology can hold those truths together.

## Repairers of the Breach

Isaiah 58 presses the question further.

The people are religiously observant. They fast, seek God, and want their devotion acknowledged. Yet God directs their attention toward oppression, hunger, homelessness, and the needs of their neighbors.

The chapter calls them to loose the bonds of wickedness, share bread with the hungry, and provide shelter and clothing. Then it offers a name for the work of restoration:

> “The repairer of the breach.”
>
> —Isaiah 58:12, KJV

The passage joins devotion to material responsibility. The community’s relationship with God must become visible in the lives of people who need relief and restoration. ([Isaiah 58, KJV](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Isaiah%2058&version=KJV))

As an Adventist, I hear a demanding invitation here. Our commitment to education expresses a conviction that the whole person matters to God. Intellectual growth, spiritual formation, character, and practical life belong together.

My family experienced the benefits of that conviction. Gratitude for our schools should deepen our concern for children beyond them.

What does it mean to become a repairer?

The image suggests more than helping someone navigate around a broken place. It asks us to attend to the break itself.

We should rescue the person in immediate danger. We should also ask why others keep encountering the same danger.

Charity and justice meet here. Food, tutoring, scholarships, and school supplies address real needs. Understanding why those needs recur can help us respond more faithfully and effectively.

Helping one child leave a harmful environment may be necessary. The question that follows is still ours:

Why must children leave in order to flourish?

## Judgment Gives Us a Defendant

There are reasons we prefer to evaluate individuals.

A parent has a name and an identifiable decision. We can consider motives, weigh consequences, and pronounce a verdict.

Institutions demand more work. Their conditions develop over years. Responsibility is distributed. Causes overlap. Improvement may require patient attention to funding, staffing, school leadership, housing, boundaries, and the experiences of families whose voices carry less influence.

Individual judgment gives us a defendant. Structural justice gives us homework.

We can spend hours debating one mother’s choices without learning much about the schools our neighbors’ children attend.

That temptation reaches me, too.

I can explain why we removed our son. I can describe the good that followed. I can remain grateful for Adventist education. None of that tells me whether I have given sufficient attention to the children whose families could not arrange a similar departure.

After I have done what is necessary for my child, what obligation remains toward the child whose parent cannot make the same choice?

I do not have a single answer that resolves every circumstance. But “my child is fine” cannot be the end of Christian concern.

## The Children Who Remain

Oliver’s request was remarkably small.

He wanted food.

Children’s ordinary educational needs should be equally recognizable: safety, competent instruction, intellectual challenge, encouragement, dignity, and the opportunity to develop their abilities.

Those goods should not depend on extraordinary parental resources.

Churches can participate in this work in concrete ways. We can tutor and mentor. We can support teachers, provide scholarships, and help families meet immediate needs. We can listen to parents in nearby public schools, including those who have few alternatives. We can attend school-board meetings, examine proposals, and ask whether resources reach children whose needs are greatest.

Listening matters before prescribing. Families who remain in a school know things that those of us who left may never have learned.

Our role is to become dependable neighbors, willing to understand both the immediate need and the conditions surrounding it.

Hannah-Jones’s story brings us to the tension experienced by a family that chose to stay. Mine brings us to a family that chose to leave. Oliver brings us to a child with almost no meaningful choice at all.

Across those different stories, one question persists:

What happens to the children who remain?

I do not regret seeking a better education for my children. I remain grateful for what Adventist education gave our family.

But gratitude has never completely erased the memory of that school in Atlanta.

We left after two days.

Other children walked back through those doors the next morning.

Christianity teaches me to ask what I owe my own children. Justice insists that I keep asking what we owe theirs.

Perhaps becoming a repairer of the breach begins by refusing to forget the children on the other side of it.
