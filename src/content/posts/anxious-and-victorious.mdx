*Paul walked away from an open door because he had no rest in his spirit—and then, in the next sentence, called his life a triumphal procession. Both sentences are inspired. Neither cancels the other.*

I want to ask you something before you read another word.

Can a person be doing exactly what God asked of him—and be so churned up inside that he cannot stay in the room where the good thing is happening? Not because of sin. Not because of unbelief. Simply because he is carrying more than one person was built to carry.

If your theology says no, you are going to have trouble with the apostle Paul.

We are living through a documented surge in this kind of distress. In the [American Psychiatric Association’s 2026 national poll](https://www.psychiatry.org/News-room/News-Releases/2026-Annual-Mental-Health-Poll), 48 percent of adults said they felt more anxious than they had the year before. In a [2022 Barna survey](https://www.barna.com/research/pastors-quitting-ministry/), 42 percent of Protestant senior pastors said they had seriously considered leaving full-time ministry within the previous year; immense stress and loneliness were among the leading reasons.

Into that moment arrives a whole Christian vocabulary of “victorious living,” often offered as though freedom from anxiety were the normal baseline of mature faith. Paul would not have made it past the first chapter of some of those books. He wrote one of his own instead. It is strange, and it is in your Bible.

## What Happened at Troas

In 2 Corinthians 2, Paul comes to Troas to preach, and “a door was opened to me by the Lord” (verse 12). It is the precise phrase every missionary prays to be able to write home.

And then he leaves.

He walks away from the open door. Why? “I had no rest in my spirit, because I did not find Titus my brother” (verse 13). Paul was waiting on news from Corinth—a congregation that had questioned his motives, resisted his authority, and bruised him badly. The not-knowing was louder than the opportunity in front of him.

Ellen White, narrating the same hour, writes that “‘the care of all the churches,’ and particularly of the church at Corinth, rested heavily on his heart” (*[The Acts of the Apostles](https://text.egwwritings.org/read/127.1427)*, p. 323).

Now read the next sentence. Same paragraph. No transition. No “but later I realized.”

“Now thanks be to God who always leads us in triumph in Christ” (verse 14).

The verb is *thriambeuō*: to lead in a triumphal procession. Every reader in the Roman world knew the picture. A victorious general returned from war, and the city staged a public triumph. Spoils moved through the streets. Incense filled the air. Captives followed the conqueror’s chariot, and for some, the procession ended in death.

Paul does not place himself on the general’s chariot. Many interpreters hear something darker and more startling in the grammar: Paul is among the conquered, being led through the streets by Christ. Other scholars understand the image differently, but the center of Paul’s claim remains: Christ is the victor, and Paul is not in charge of the procession.

The fragrance filling the air is “the fragrance of the knowledge of Him” (verse 14), carried by people whose lives have been conquered by grace.

That is Paul’s picture of victory.

Led, not leading.

And in the same man, in the same week, in the same breath: *no rest in my spirit*.

Go back one chapter and the tension sharpens. “We were burdened beyond measure, above strength, so that we despaired even of life” (2 Corinthians 1:8). The man who wrote “rejoice in the Lord always” also wrote “we despaired even of life.” Both are Scripture. Both are true.

Faith is not the eraser you take to the second sentence.

## Two Ways We Grade Faith by Feeling

Somewhere along the way, we started reading a person’s emotional temperature and calling it a spiritual diagnosis. The mistake usually appears in two forms.

The first is **peace as thermometer**.

“The peace of God, which surpasses all understanding” gets quietly recast as a mood you are responsible to maintain. Its absence then becomes evidence against you: feel the churn, fail the test.

But Philippians 4 does not promise the sensation of calm. It promises that God’s peace “will guard your hearts and minds through Christ Jesus” (verse 7). The image is not sedation. It is a sentry posted around you while the assault continues.

The guard on the wall is not proof that the war has ended. It is proof that you are not on the wall alone.

The second is **equanimity mistaken for anesthesia**.

In medicine, we prize equanimity. In his 1889 address *Aequanimitas*, William Osler commended “imperturbability”—[calmness in the middle of danger](https://www.ams-inc.on.ca/wp-content/uploads/2018/08/Sir-William-Osler-English.pdf). But clinical calm is not numbness. It is the trained discipline of staying fully present to a hemorrhage without letting fear make the decision.

The counterfeit says: care less, want less, love less, and you will hurt less.

Scripture asks for no such thing. Paul loved the Corinthians enough that their silence could pull him bodily out of an open door. The ache was the interest owed on the love.

Underneath both errors is an Adventist frame that helps us see the matter more clearly. If the great controversy is real—if a genuine conflict between Christ and evil runs beneath human history—then the people nearest the front may feel the shelling. Calm is not always the fruit of faith. Sometimes it is simply distance from the fighting.

The soldier in the forward trench and the traveler three countries away are having very different nights. Only one of them is where the battle is.

At Troas, Paul was in the trench.

## The Two Rooms Where I Know This

I know this from both of my rooms.

I practice maternal-fetal medicine. I carry pregnancies that may not end the way every person in the room is praying they will. I have taken the 2 a.m. call. I have walked out of a delivery that went wrong and been expected in clinic at eight—composed, useful, fully present for the next family, who have no idea what the previous hour held.

Then I have lain awake that night, running the film backward, asking whether a different decision an hour earlier would have changed the ending.

For a long time, I read that wakefulness as a spiritual problem. A man of real faith would sleep. A man who genuinely trusted God would not still be doing the math at three in the morning.

I have come to think that was bad theology wearing the costume of humility.

The churn was not necessarily the failure of my faith. It was the price of not going numb—of refusing the anesthesia that might make the work easier tonight and make me a worse physician tomorrow.

It is the same in the eldership. You come to know things about people that you cannot fix. You carry them into the week. You sit in a board meeting holding what you know and cannot say. The temptation is to read your own disquiet as a disqualification—to assume that the mature elder has somehow arranged to feel less.

He has not.

He has learned, like Paul at Troas, to keep speaking about Christ with the disquiet still sitting in his chest. Ellen White noticed this tenderness in Paul: when correction was necessary, “it cost him pain to give them pain” (*[SDA Bible Commentary](https://m.egwwritings.org/en/book/95.533)*, vol. 6, p. 1094).

The pain was not the flaw in his ministry. It was evidence that love had not left it.

## What Faithful Comfort Looks Like

Stop using the feeling of peace as a spiritual thermometer—on yourself, and especially on other people.

The believer white-knuckling through a panic attack in the third pew does not need a verdict about trust. He needs what the Greek of 2 Corinthians 1 holds out: *paraklēsis*, comfort that comes alongside. Sit down next to him. Help him find appropriate care. Stay after the benediction.

Let the comfort move through you. Paul says that God comforts us “that we may be able to comfort those who are in any trouble” with the comfort we have received from Him (2 Corinthians 1:4). The comfort you were given in your worst year was never meant to terminate with you.

Ellen White saw the same movement: “Those who have borne the greatest sorrows are frequently the ones who carry the greatest comfort to others” (*[God’s Amazing Grace](https://m.egwwritings.org/en/book/33.851)*, p. 122).

Your scar may become someone else’s map.

If you lead anything, tell your people the truth about your own churn. Paul did. “We despaired even of life” remains in the inspired text on purpose. A congregation that never sees its leaders shake will conclude that shaking disqualifies them, and every anxious member in the building will suffer in silence under that verdict.

And put the victory back where Paul put it.

Not in your circumstances. Not in your pulse rate. Not in your nervous system.

In Christ, at the head of a procession you did not organize and are not leading.

You are being led through the street. That is the good news and the hard news folded into a single image.

Anxious and victorious. Both. At once.

The parade is real. So are the chains.

Faith was never the absence of the tremor. It is Whose hand you are trembling in.
