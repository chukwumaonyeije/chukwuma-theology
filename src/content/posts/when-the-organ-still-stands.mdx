*A Church, a Hotel, and the Grace of the Great Controversy*

I am writing from Utrecht, where I have come for a medical conference.

My hotel used to be a church.

It is called Bunk now. For more than a century it was the Westerkerk, a Reformed church built in 1891 on the edge of the old city. Its [last worship service was held on January 6, 2018](https://www.gergeminfo.nl/nieuwsberichten/bij-het-verlaten-van-de-westerkerk). In June 2019, it reopened as a hotel.

Where pews once stood, travelers gather for meals. A great wooden block houses rooms, a bar, and a kitchen inside the former sanctuary. According to [Bunk’s account of the conversion](https://wearebunk.com/nl/press/bunk-utrecht-fact-sheet/), its paneling was made from the old church benches.

But the architecture remembers.

The wooden barrel vault still arches overhead. Light still falls through windows designed for worship. And on the wall above the room, half hidden behind the hotel block, the organ still stands.

---

## The Organ Is Not Silent

I assumed it was a relic.

It is not.

The Quellhorst instrument is more than two hundred years old. It came from the Catholic church of St. Plechelmus in Oldenzaal and was transferred to Utrecht in 1893. The Dutch [cultural heritage register](https://kennis.cultureelerfgoed.nl/index.php/Monumenten/498263) records its history and protected status. Bunk has kept it playable and [continues its life through evening concerts](https://wearebunk.com/utrecht/history/).

Two carved angels sit atop the case, trumpets raised.

The instrument has served Catholic worship and Reformed worship. It now serves a different kind of gathering: listeners assembled for a concert in a hotel.

That detail unsettled me more than silence would have.

A silent organ is a ruin. You can mourn a ruin.

An organ that still plays beautifully in a room no longer gathered for congregational worship raises a different question. The instrument retains its voice. The room has changed its purpose.

That does not tell me what is happening in every listener’s heart. A concert can awaken gratitude, memory, even prayer. I cannot read a stranger’s soul from a breakfast table. But I can recognize the difference between an audience gathered to hear music and a congregation gathered to praise God.

---

## The Easy Sermon

There is an obvious sermon to preach in a building like this.

It is a sermon about European secularization, and it is not wrong. Across this continent, churches that once anchored community life have become apartments, bookstores, concert halls, and hotels. The buildings remain. The worshipers have thinned.

Standing in that room, I could not avoid thinking about the Great Controversy.

For Seventh-day Adventists, the Great Controversy is more than a prophetic timeline. At its heart, it is a cosmic conflict over the character of God.

Can He be trusted?
Is His way good?
Is love stronger than coercion?
Is humanity better off with Him or without Him?

Those questions are not confined to theology books. They surface in the choices of people, institutions, and cultures, including the decision to organize life with little reference to God.

A church becoming a hotel can feel like a verdict.

The sacred becomes secular.
Worship becomes commerce.
The sanctuary becomes accommodation.

It would be easy to stand there and lament.

But one fact complicated my sermon.

The congregation that worshiped here did not simply disappear. Its [own history](https://gergemutrecht.nl/geschiedenis/) describes declining membership and high maintenance costs, followed by a move to the Nieuwe Westerkerk in Leidsche Rijn in 2019. There was real contraction, and there was continuing faith. The people left the building. They did not abandon worship.

As a physician, I know the danger of allowing one striking finding to become the whole diagnosis. A converted church is a fact. The spiritual condition of everyone connected with it is a much larger story.

And that pushed me toward a harder question.

Not **What happened to them?**

But **What has been entrusted to us?**

---

## Knowing God Is Itself Grace

Christians sometimes talk about secular society as if we stand safely on one side of a divide, watching everyone else drift away.

The gospel gives us no ground for that posture.

If I know Christ, that knowledge is a gift.

If Scripture shaped my imagination, if hymns put the language of faith in my mouth before I understood it, if parents and teachers and pastors introduced me to Jesus, I did not manufacture any of that.

Jesus sent His disciples out with this instruction:

"Freely ye have received, freely give." Matthew 10:8

That verse changes how I see the travelers eating breakfast beneath that organ.

Some may be Christians themselves. Others may never have encountered a compelling account of Christ. They may know Christianity mainly through its scandals, its political fights, its institutional failures, its hypocrisy.

They have heard a great deal about Christians.

They may have heard very little that sounded like good news.

If that is true, our first responsibility is not to condemn the darkness.

It is to carry light.

---

## We Must Be Careful Here

This is not an argument for softening the truth.

The Great Controversy is real. Scripture names a deceiver and takes human estrangement from God seriously. Yet it also locates pride, falsehood, and spiritual resistance among religious people. A church address gives none of us immunity.

But raw power cannot establish the truth of God’s goodness in the hearts of His creatures. Power can silence an accuser. It cannot, by itself, create trust. Within the Great Controversy, God’s answer includes allowing His character to be seen in the history of His dealings with us.

He answers by revealing His character.

The definitive answer is Jesus.

"He that hath seen me hath seen the Father." John 14:9

At Calvary, God does not merely announce that He loves the world. He demonstrates it. The God accused of selfishness gives Himself. The God accused of tyranny gives Himself for His enemies. This does not remove judgment from the biblical story. It shows us the character of the One who judges.

That means our witness cannot consist mainly of announcing that society has become secular. Everyone already knows that.

Our lives have to make the character of God visible.

Sometimes that happens through preaching.

It also happens through hospitality.
Through justice.
Through forgiveness.
Through intellectual honesty.
Through caring for the sick.
Through listening to people who disagree with us as people made in the image of God.

The great challenge facing Christians in a secular age may not be convincing people that God exists.

It may be helping them imagine that God is good.

---

## An Organ Needs Breath

I kept looking at that organ.

An organ is a strange instrument. Its pipes can be perfect, its case magnificent, its history distinguished. None of that makes a sound.

It needs breath.

Air moves through the pipes, and only then does structure become music.

There is theology hiding there.

Buildings can remain.
Institutions can remain.
Doctrines can remain.
Schools, hospitals, and denominations can remain.

Structures alone cannot proclaim the gospel.

They need breath.

Scripture keeps returning to that image.

"And the LORD God formed man of the dust of the ground, and breathed into his nostrils the breath of life; and man became a living soul." Genesis 2:7

After the resurrection, Jesus stood among His frightened disciples:

"He breathed on them, and saith unto them, Receive ye the Holy Ghost." John 20:22

And at Pentecost:

"Suddenly there came a sound from heaven as of a rushing mighty wind, and it filled all the house where they were sitting." Acts 2:2

The church has always depended on more than architecture. It depends on the breath of God moving through human beings.

The Bunk organ reminds me that a mechanism can function after its original communal purpose has changed. The analogy has limits: the Holy Spirit is a divine Person, not an impersonal force, and musical beauty is no evidence of His absence. Still, the image turns the question toward me.

The question for me is whether the same is possible in a Christian life. Can I keep the structure, the doctrine, the attendance, even the music, while the Spirit no longer moves through me?

That question did not stay in Utrecht’s public life. It followed me upstairs.

I can defend the character of God in an essay and misrepresent Him in a conversation. I can explain religious liberty while becoming impatient with someone who uses that liberty to disagree with me. I can speak accurately about grace and make another person feel that my respect must be earned.

These are not failures of theological vocabulary. They are failures of surrender. Being able to describe the Spirit’s work is different from consenting to it.

Paul makes the test painfully concrete: love, joy, peace, longsuffering, gentleness, goodness, faith, meekness, and temperance (Galatians 5:22–23). The fruit is recognizable in how we treat people, especially when they inconvenience us. Correct doctrine matters because it tells the truth about God. The Spirit brings that truth to bear on the person who professes it.

Before I ask why a secular neighbor does not want my faith, I need to ask what that neighbor has been able to see of Christ through me.

---

## The Church Had No Cultural Power Once Before

Christians can meet secularization with fear.

We can retreat.
We can grow angry.
We can romanticize an era when the pews were full and the church had influence.

Or we can recognize the missionary moment in front of us.

The gospel has never depended on Christianity holding cultural power.

The first Christians had almost none. They carried the message of Jesus through an empire that misunderstood them, marginalized them, and at times executed them.

The New Testament describes communities learning to live by another allegiance. In Acts 4:32–35, believers share resources so that the needy are cared for. In Acts 10, Peter has to learn that the boundaries he inherited cannot determine whom God receives. In Acts 16, an imprisoned Paul and Silas speak the word of the Lord to their jailer and his household.

These communities were imperfect. Paul’s letters leave no room to romanticize them. Yet the gospel traveled through their witness without waiting for the empire’s permission or approval.

For Adventists, Revelation 14 places the everlasting gospel before every nation, kindred, tongue, and people. Its summons to worship the Creator gives our mission urgency. That urgency should make us more attentive to actual people: their questions, their wounds, and their freedom to respond.

The secular cities of Europe and North America may not be only evidence of Christianity's decline.

They may be mission fields.

Not territory to conquer.

People to love.

---

## The Church Outside the Church

There is one more irony in sleeping inside a converted sanctuary.

The building still gathers people.

Strangers sit at long tables together. Meals are shared. Travelers find shelter. Conversations in a dozen languages rise toward the old vaulted ceiling.

The building still makes room for human encounter. Hospitality here is a real good, even when it is not offered as an act of Christian ministry.

That is a lesson for the church.

The gospel was never meant to stay inside our sanctuaries.

"Go ye therefore, and teach all nations, baptizing them in the name of the Father, and of the Son, and of the Holy Ghost." Matthew 28:19

**Go.**

Not wait for them to come.

When a church becomes a hotel, Christians may rightly feel sadness. But we should also hear a summons. The congregation that worshiped here carried its faith out of this building and planted it in a new neighborhood. Their move reminds me that Christian life can continue beyond a beloved address. The Great Commission asks still more of us: to make disciples, baptize, and teach people to live in the way Jesus commanded.

The gospel has to leave the building.

It has to enter hospitals and universities, software companies and coffee shops, neighborhoods and conference halls.

It has to travel with us.

At a medical conference, that may begin with listening carefully to a colleague who carries a painful history with religion. At home, it may mean inviting a neighbor to a meal without treating the invitation as a sales pitch. In a congregation, it may mean answering a difficult question honestly, including the words “I do not know,” and then opening Scripture together.

Our conduct cannot tell the whole gospel by itself; people also need to hear who Jesus is and what He has done. But our conduct can make that conversation credible. We have received something worth giving freely.

---

## When the Organ Still Stands

Tonight, below my room, the old organ stands on its wall with its two angels and their raised trumpets.

Its next concert will gather another audience. Afterward, the room will return to meals and conversation. The music’s beauty will remain a gift, even though this is no longer the congregation’s home.

There is something melancholy in that.

But I do not want melancholy to have the last word.

The gospel has never depended on one building.

The church is not stone or stained glass or pipes. The church is a people who have received grace and have been sent to share it.

So this is what I will carry home from an unusual hotel in Utrecht:

When the organ remains in the building, the people of God must carry the song into the world.

Not louder.
Not angrier.
Not more afraid.

More faithful.
More gracious.
More loving.

More willing to tell a world that may have forgotten God, or may never have truly known Him, that He is still good.

And that His grace is still freely given.

"Freely ye have received, freely give." Matthew 10:8

## Questions for Reflection

- When you see a closed or converted church, is your first instinct grief, judgment, or curiosity? What does that reveal about how you view the people outside it?
- Why does the Great Controversy require God to answer Satan's accusations by revealing His character rather than by using force?
- What does it mean to "keep the structure" of Christian life while losing the breath of the Spirit? Where might that be true for you?
- The early church spread without cultural power. Which of their practices could your congregation recover this year?
- Name one place in your weekly life, outside any church building, where the character of God could become visible through you.
