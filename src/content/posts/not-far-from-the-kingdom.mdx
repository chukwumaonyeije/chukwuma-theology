I want to think carefully about a survey result that should unsettle every one of us who takes doctrine seriously.

Pew Research Center's most recent Religious Landscape Study, released in February 2025, found something surprising: the decades-long decline in American Christianity has leveled off. Sixty-two percent of U.S. adults still call themselves Christian. That's actually the encouraging headline. Here's the one that stopped me. Among the roughly one-third of Americans who left the faith they were raised in, a meaningful share — about a third — point to scandals involving clergy or religious leaders as part of why they walked away. Not primarily because they stopped believing in God. Because they watched people who claimed to know God behave in ways that made the claim unbelievable.

Let me begin where all good diagnosis begins: with the presenting symptom. The symptom is not atheism. The symptom is credibility collapse. People are not mainly abandoning the idea of God. They are abandoning the idea that religious people can be trusted to embody what they preach.

I don't think this is a new disease. I think it's the oldest one in Scripture, and Mark 12 gives us the clearest diagnostic image of it I know.

## A Gauntlet of Bad-Faith Questions

By the time we reach verse 28, Jesus has already survived an ambush. The Pharisees and Herodians tried to trap him on taxes. The Sadducees, who didn't even believe in the resurrection, tried to make the whole idea look absurd with an elaborate hypothetical about a widow and seven brothers. Every question in this chapter up to this point is a performance. Nobody is actually trying to learn anything. They are trying to win.

And then, almost as an aside, a scribe steps forward. Mark tells us why: he had listened to Jesus answer them well. Not to trap him. To ask him something real: "Which commandment is the most important of all?"

Jesus gives him the Shema first — "Hear, O Israel: the Lord our God, the Lord is one" — and then, without missing a beat, Leviticus 19:18: love your neighbor as yourself. And the scribe, remarkably, doesn't argue. He agrees, and then he goes further than agreement. He says this is worth more than all the burnt offerings and sacrifices in the temple where he is presumably standing.

I want to spend more time here than the text seems to ask for, because I think this is the moment the whole passage turns on.

## The Shema Is Not a Slogan

We tend to read "the Lord is one" as a tidy monotheistic checkbox — there's one God, not many, moving on. But the Hebrew word *echad* carries more weight than mere counting. It signals unity, undividedness. This is not simply an arithmetic claim about God's number. It's a claim about God's character: undivided, unrivaled, without competing loyalties. And the text moves immediately — almost without a breath — from that claim to a command: therefore, love this God with all your heart, all your soul, all your might. Because God is undivided in his faithfulness to us, our love toward him is meant to be equally undivided.

This is the deepest question underneath the Great Controversy, the cosmic conflict that Adventist theology insists is not decorative background but the actual architecture of the biblical story. The whole war being fought — in heaven before time, and in every human heart since — is over whether this kind of God, this kind of government, actually deserves total allegiance. The Shema is Israel's answer to that question, centuries before the question is fully put to the test in the wilderness, in the monarchy, at the cross.

And then Leviticus 19:18 turns that same undivided love outward. Read in its original context, this command doesn't float in the air as sentiment. It sits inside a chapter packed with concrete social ethics — leave the edges of your field for the poor, pay your worker before sundown, don't cheat with unequal weights, don't hate your brother in your heart. Love of neighbor was never abstract. It was gleaning laws and honest wages. This is why Jesus can join these two commands — one vertical, one horizontal — into a single answer. They were never two commandments competing for the same space. They were one love, pointed in two directions.

## Saul: The Diagnosis of the Counterfeit

Here is where I want to bring in a text five hundred years older than the scribe's temple courtyard. First Samuel 15. Saul has been commanded to devote the Amalekites' plunder to complete destruction — obey the specific word of the Lord, full stop. Instead, he keeps the best of the flocks and then offers them as sacrifice. And Samuel's response is one of the sharpest lines in the Old Testament:

> "Has the Lord as great delight in burnt offerings and sacrifices, as in obeying the voice of the Lord? Behold, to obey is better than sacrifice, and to listen than the fat of rams."
> — 1 Samuel 15:22, ESV

I used to read this as a comment about ritual versus ethics. I don't think that's quite right anymore. I think it's a comment about government — about whose will is actually on the throne. Saul is not irreligious. He's very religious. He offers the sacrifice. He invokes the Lord's name. What he will not do is submit his will to a command he didn't like. This is the character of the original rebellion, dressed in worship clothes. Lucifer never denied God's existence. He resisted God's right to define the terms.

A sacrifice offered on your own terms is not an offering. It's a negotiation.

## Two Ways the Counterfeit Shows Up Today

This same pattern — religious form surviving while the will underneath keeps its own throne — still splits in two directions, and I think both are visible in why a third of the people who left their childhood faith cite clergy scandal as part of the reason.

The first is **vertical religion without mercy**. This is the crusader — theologically precise, doctrinally airtight, fiercely protective of Sabbath and prophecy and every fine point of belief, and simultaneously harsh, judgmental, cold toward the broken people standing right in front of them. This is the absolute tragedy of correct belief weaponized by a bitter spirit. And within our own tradition, I don't think we get to pretend this danger belongs to someone else. It's easy to hold flawless command of the sanctuary doctrine, the prophetic timeline, the Sabbath, and use all of it as a wall instead of a window.

The second is **horizontal concern without worship**. This is the drift I see most often outside religious circles now, and it shows up right in that Pew data — the growing number of "nones" who still hold spiritual beliefs, who still care about justice and kindness and community, but who have quietly detached all of it from any recognition of divine authority, any personal holiness, any surrender to a King. It is, as I've heard it put well, an attempt to build the kingdom of God without the King.

Jesus's synthesis in Mark 12 refuses both amputations. You cannot sever the vertical from the horizontal without destroying the shape of the cross itself, which was always a vertical beam and a horizontal beam, fastened together, holding one body.

## Not Far Is Still Outside

And now we arrive at the line that should unsettle every one of us who has ever felt satisfied with our own theology. The scribe gets it right. He hears the Shema and Leviticus 19:18 named as the greatest commandments, and instead of merely nodding, he ranks them above sacrifice — a striking, almost prophetic move from inside the very system that depended on sacrifice for its legitimacy. And Jesus looks at him and says:

> "You are not far from the kingdom of God."
> — Mark 12:34, ESV

When I first sat with this line, I read it as a passing grade. I thought: good, he answered correctly, he's in. But not far is a geography, and geography still means outside. Why doesn't Jesus simply welcome him in?

Because Jesus is drawing a distinction here that I think should terrify anyone raised in a deeply theological home. The distinction between intellectual assent and personal surrender. The scribe has correctly diagnosed the moral center of the universe. He has the map exactly right. But analyzing the truth is not the same thing as submitting your life to the Truth standing in front of you in the flesh. This is the difference between studying the contour lines of a mountain and actually shouldering a pack and walking the trail. You can memorize every ridge and describe the summit to anyone who asks, and you still haven't climbed anything.

## The Consult I Got Right

I want to tell you about the appointment I got everything right.

A patient came to see me after an ultrasound flagged a serious fetal anomaly — one of the harder conversations in my specialty. I walked into that room and did exactly what fifteen years of training built into me. I explained the finding in plain language. I laid out the differential, the confirmatory testing, the range of outcomes, the next steps — in the right order, at the right pace, anticipating her questions before she asked them. My documentation afterward was clean. Every box the standard of care requires, checked. If you audited that chart, you would find nothing to correct.

Her husband caught me in the hallway on my way to the next room. He didn't ask about the anomaly. He asked, "Did you notice she stopped hearing you around minute four?"

I hadn't noticed. I had been so precise about the medicine that I missed the exact moment the room stopped being a consultation and became grief.

I have replayed that hallway more than I've replayed almost any diagnosis I've made in twenty years of practice. Because I had gotten the map exactly right, and I had missed the person standing on it.

I tell you that story because I don't get to read the scribe's near-miss from a safe distance. I can walk a Sabbath School class through the sanctuary, the investigative judgment, the state of the dead, the Sabbath as sign and seal, with the same clean fluency I use to walk a frightened mother through a fetal MRI — completely, correctly, without a wasted word — and still leave the room without having actually loved the person in front of me. Correct information delivered by an unmoved heart is not yet love. It's just accurate.

This is not a hypothetical danger for a tradition like ours, built as it is on doctrinal precision. Ellen White returned to this warning again and again — what she called legal religion, the temptation to let exact obedience quietly substitute for a surrendered will. Saul's failure in the wilderness was never too little religion. It was religion with the wrong person still governing the room. And I have to ask it of myself in both rooms I work in, the clinic and the church board table: am I delivering information, or am I present? Because from where the patient sits, and from where the congregation sits, a technically perfect encounter and a genuinely loving one can look identical for the first three minutes. Only what happens in minute four tells you which one it was.

## The Practical Close

So what do we actually do with this — those of us who love doctrine, who love the map, who could recite the whole territory from memory?

We let the map do what maps are for: get us walking, not let us stop at admiring the terrain. Concretely, that means testing every doctrinal conviction against a simple question before we let ourselves feel settled in it: has this made me more merciful, or only more certain? Vertical without mercy and horizontal without worship are both, in the end, ways of stopping short of the trailhead while insisting we've already summited.

The scribe stood closer to the kingdom than almost anyone else in that courtyard that day. Correct doctrine got him to the threshold. It could not walk him through the door. Only surrender does that.

Put down the map. Pick up the pack. Start walking.
