There is a way to win an argument and lose something more important.

You finally deliver the sentence that silences the person who wounded you. You expose the weakness, return the humiliation, and watch the room turn in your favor. Everything about the moment feels like victory—until you recognize how much your conduct now resembles the conduct you once condemned.

Evil has more than one way of winning. Sometimes it defeats us by injuring us. Sometimes it recruits us by persuading us that its methods are necessary.

Paul addresses that second danger with extraordinary economy:

<div class="scripture">
  "Do not be overcome by evil, but overcome evil with good."
  <cite>Romans 12:21, NKJV</cite>
</div>

This is more demanding than a request to be pleasant. Paul is asking what kind of people we become when we encounter wrongdoing. Will another person's hatred begin to write our character? Or will the mercy of God produce a response that bears the likeness of Christ?

The issue reaches beyond temperament. It concerns the gospel's power to transform the injured without excusing the injury.

## When Evil Begins to Set the Terms

The Greek verb behind both commands is *nikaō*: to conquer, prevail, or overcome. Paul first uses a passive command, then an active one. Do not be conquered. Conquer.

The present imperatives fit an ongoing way of life. They do not, by themselves, prove that Paul's readers were already surrendering to evil and needed to stop. Their force is nevertheless searching: this is a pattern of conduct Christians must continually resist and continually practice.

Being harmed by evil and being overcome by evil are not identical experiences.

A person can suffer injustice without becoming unjust. A believer can receive hatred without making hatred the governing principle of the next decision. Grief, anger, and the need for protection do not themselves mean that evil has won. The danger appears when the wrong begins to supply our moral instructions.

Someone lies about us, and we begin to justify deception. Someone treats us cruelly, and we decide cruelty is the only language that person understands. Someone abuses authority, and we dream of possessing enough authority to make them feel equally helpless.

The grievance may be legitimate. The imitation remains dangerous.

Paul refuses to let the person who injured us become the architect of our response. Following Christ means that even under pressure, our conduct has another source.

## The Verse Has a Neighborhood

Romans 12:21 concludes an argument about sincere love. Verse 9 tells believers to abhor evil and cling to good. Verse 21 tells them to overcome evil with good. Those related commands frame a passage filled with remarkably concrete instructions.

Honor one another. Practice hospitality. Bless persecutors. Weep with those who weep. Refuse pride. Do not repay evil for evil.

Then Paul adds a qualification that deserves to be heard whenever reconciliation is discussed:

<div class="scripture">
  "If it is possible, as much as depends on you, live peaceably with all men."
  <cite>Romans 12:18, NKJV</cite>
</div>

Paul recognizes limits. Peace involves other people's choices. We are responsible for our faithfulness; we cannot guarantee another person's cooperation.

His next instruction places vengeance in God's hands. This does not mean wrongdoing has become unimportant. It means the believer does not occupy the seat of final judgment. God has neither overlooked the wound nor surrendered His moral government of the world.

That trust creates room for a startling action. Drawing from Proverbs 25:21–22, Paul tells Christians to feed a hungry enemy and give water to a thirsty one. The enemy remains an enemy in the sentence. Kindness does not depend on pretending the hostility never happened.

The reference to burning coals in verse 20 has been understood in different ways, including shame that may lead to repentance and an image connected with judgment. Whatever interpretation one adopts, Paul's prohibition of vengeance rules out treating kindness as a clever method of causing pain.

Food and water are gifts, not disguised weapons of humiliation.

The concluding command gathers the passage together: resist evil through a goodness that refuses to reproduce it.

## Goodness Has Something to Do

Paul's second command is active. The Christian response cannot end with keeping resentment behind a polite expression.

The phrase translated "with good" identifies the means of overcoming. Goodness becomes the way believers confront evil. It has work to perform.

Sometimes that work is as ordinary as refusing to circulate a damaging story. Sometimes it means providing practical help to someone who would never help us. Sometimes it requires a difficult conversation, an honest report, or a firm boundary that interrupts harm.

There is nothing passive about telling the truth when concealment would be easier. There is nothing weak about protecting someone whose suffering powerful people would prefer to ignore.

But the command does not promise that every generous act will change an enemy's heart. Jesus loved perfectly, and people still rejected Him. Paul is describing faithful resistance, not offering a technique for controlling outcomes.

We may lose influence, comfort, or a relationship while remaining faithful to this verse. The goodness of our response cannot be measured solely by whether an opponent becomes agreeable. Its first measure is whether it reflects the Lord we follow.

## Mercy Comes Before the Command

Romans 12 begins with an appeal grounded in "the mercies of God." That opening prevents us from turning Christian ethics into a program of heroic self-improvement.

Paul has already described humanity's sin, justification by faith, union with Christ, life in the Spirit, and God's faithfulness. Now he describes the life that answers those mercies. The renewed mind of Romans 12:2 belongs to the same argument as the nonretaliating love of Romans 12:21.

We do not become good enough to persuade God to show mercy. His mercy meets us in Christ and begins to change us.

For Adventists, this order matters deeply. Obedience is the fruit of salvation received through faith, never a substitute for it. Sanctification includes our habits of speech, our handling of anger, and our use of power. Yet none of that growth supplies the ground of our acceptance with God.

A wounded person does not need another burden disguised as a sermon: *Prove you are a Christian by feeling no pain.* The gospel offers a Savior who receives the wounded and a Spirit who works patiently within them.

Often the beginning is a prayer spoken before any generous feeling arrives: Lord, keep this wrong from teaching me to do wrong. Show me the next faithful action.

## The Cross Gives the Command Its Shape

Jesus' teaching about loving enemies rests on the Father's character. In Matthew 5:43–48, sunshine and rain become witnesses to divine benevolence. God gives good things even to those who resist Him.

Paul grounds the same pattern in the gospel itself. According to Romans 5:6–10, Christ died for us while we were sinners, and reconciliation reached us while we were enemies. God's love did not wait for hostility to disappear before moving toward its object.

At Calvary, that love entered the full violence of human rejection. The innocent One was condemned. Authority served expediency. Mockery accompanied suffering.

Yet human hatred could not make Christ hateful. First Peter 2:23 describes Him refusing retaliation and entrusting Himself to the One who judges righteously. His trust in divine justice and His refusal of revenge belonged together.

The resurrection reveals that violence did not have the final word. Through Christ's death and resurrection, God acts decisively against sin and death. Christian goodness rests on that victory; it does not have to manufacture it.

This also keeps us from using the cross irresponsibly. Christ's unique saving sacrifice is never permission to demand that vulnerable people remain available to an abuser. Following the crucified Lord includes caring for those He loves, telling the truth about violence, and refusing to sanctify another person's cruelty.

## The Great Controversy in Ordinary Life

The Adventist understanding of the great controversy places human conduct within a larger conflict over God's character and government. Evil misrepresents God. Christ reveals Him.

In chapter 79 of *The Desire of Ages*, "It Is Finished," Ellen White reflects on the cross as the disclosure of God's character and the exposure of Satan's rebellion. Calvary reveals the moral difference between self-giving love and the will to dominate.

Romans 12:21 brings that difference into ordinary relationships.

A church board can defend a correct position through intimidation. A parent can teach obedience through humiliation. A believer can speak about God's justice while enjoying the prospect of an opponent's suffering. In each case, a religious claim may coexist with conduct that obscures the character of Christ.

This does not make every disagreement a direct confrontation between Christ and Satan. It does make our methods theologically significant. The way we pursue a good end can contradict the God we say we represent.

God's love includes judgment and accountability. His goodness is morally serious. That is precisely why Christian resistance must seek truth, protection, and restoration without embracing cruelty.

## Goodness Protects the Vulnerable

No reading of this verse should silence a victim.

Paul distinguishes personal vengeance from justice. Immediately after his instructions about nonretaliation, Romans 13 discusses governing authorities and their responsibility to restrain wrongdoing. That passage raises substantial interpretive questions, but its proximity makes one point clear: refusing private revenge does not erase accountability.

Reporting abuse, leaving danger, establishing boundaries, and seeking appropriate legal protection can be expressions of goodness. Forgiveness does not automatically restore trust or return someone to leadership. Reconciliation requires truth and cannot be imposed by the person who caused the harm.

Church leaders therefore have a particular responsibility. We must never ask a wounded person to carry the cost of an institution's reputation. Feeding an enemy and concealing an offender are profoundly different actions.

The moral question is whether our response protects life and pursues what is right while refusing personal vengeance. Goodness can be tender toward a sufferer and immovable toward the behavior that threatens them.

## Two Islands, One Searching Question

William Golding's *Lord of the Flies* imagines boys whose island community descends into fear, domination, and violence. The society that should protect them becomes a source of terror.

The historical account of six Tongan boys stranded on the island of ʻAta in 1965 offers a different illustration. Rescued in 1966, they survived through cooperation, shared responsibilities, and care for one another. Their experience challenges the assumption that crisis must always dissolve community into savagery.

The comparison has limits. A novel and a historical survival account cannot settle the doctrine of human nature. The Tongan boys' conduct does not prove sinlessness, establish sanctification, or allow us to map the precise operation of grace in their lives.

But the contrast helps us see a question Paul presses upon every community: what principles will govern us when life becomes frightening?

We do not need a deserted island to find out. A family crisis, a congregational dispute, or a public injustice can expose the same temptations. Fear can make domination seem reasonable. Hurt can make revenge sound righteous.

Romans 12:21 opens another path, made possible by God's transforming mercy.

Evil is contagious, but it is not irresistible. Goodness is active, and it must remain joined to truth. Victory takes its shape from Christ, whose love endured hostility without becoming its reflection.

The next act may be small: an honest word, a protected boundary, a meal, a refusal to humiliate, an apology without excuses. Such acts do not settle every conflict. They do bear witness to another kingdom.

The person who harmed you does not have to become the author of who you are becoming.

Christ has already called you to a different likeness.
