*Judas, Silas, Anna, Philip’s daughters, and what their ministries teach us about Scripture, spiritual gifts, and Ellen G. White.*

Imagine hearing someone say, “God has given a message to His church.”

For many Christians, that statement immediately raises questions. Is the person claiming to speak with the authority of the Bible? Are we being asked to accept new doctrine? If Scripture already gives us the foundation of Christian faith, why would the church need a prophetic message today?

These are reasonable questions. Religious history contains enough mistaken predictions, manipulative leaders, and competing claims of revelation to make caution necessary. A person’s confidence that God has spoken does not establish that God has actually spoken.

Yet caution raises another question: Does protecting the authority of Scripture require us to deny the possibility of any prophetic ministry after the Bible was completed?

For Seventh-day Adventists, this question has particular importance. The church affirms the Bible as the supreme standard of faith and practice while also recognizing the ministry of Ellen G. White as a manifestation of the gift of prophecy. To someone encountering Adventist beliefs for the first time, these commitments may seem difficult to reconcile.

If Ellen White was a prophet, why are her writings not included in the Bible? And if her writings are not Scripture, in what sense can they carry prophetic authority?

Before considering Ellen White, we need to understand a distinction already present within the Bible itself.

The word *canon* refers to the recognized collection of books that make up Scripture. When Christians speak about the biblical canon, they mean the books received as the church’s authoritative written standard. Seventh-day Adventists recognize the sixty-six books of the Old and New Testaments.

A *prophet*, meanwhile, is someone through whom God communicates. Biblical prophets sometimes predicted future events, but their work also included warning, correction, encouragement, and directing people toward faithfulness.

These two categories overlap, but they are not identical. Some prophets contributed writings that became part of Scripture. Others exercised genuine prophetic ministries without leaving us a biblical book—or even a recorded message.

Calling someone a *noncanonical prophet* therefore does not mean that the person was a false prophet. It means that the person’s prophetic ministry did not produce a separate writing included in the biblical canon. Some of these prophets appear within Scripture, even though their own messages are largely or entirely unrecorded.

That distinction helps us ask the central question more carefully: Must every genuine prophet become an author of Scripture?

The Bible gives us good reasons to answer no.

Consider Judas Barsabbas and Silas.

Their story appears during a difficult moment in the early church. Christians were debating what should be required of Gentiles who came to faith in Jesus. After the Jerusalem Council addressed the dispute, Judas and Silas accompanied Paul and Barnabas to communicate its decision to believers in Antioch.

Luke writes:

> “And Judas and Silas, being prophets also themselves, exhorted the brethren with many words, and confirmed them.”
>
> — Acts 15:32, KJV

Luke explicitly identifies both men as prophets. Their ministry strengthened and encouraged the church during a time when misunderstanding could have damaged its unity.

Yet Luke does not give us a transcript of their “many words.”

We know that these men were prophets. We know that their ministry benefited believers. But we do not possess the messages they delivered on that occasion.

Their example shows that a person’s prophetic ministry did not depend on every message being preserved for all future generations. God could use a prophet to meet the needs of a particular community at a particular moment.

We should also be precise about what this passage establishes. Luke does not say that every sentence Judas and Silas spoke was a direct revelation. Identifying someone as a prophet does not turn all of that person’s ordinary conversation into inspired speech. Nevertheless, their recognized prophetic role is clear.

Anna provides another example.

In Luke’s account of Jesus’ infancy, Anna is identified as a prophetess. She was an elderly widow whose life was devoted to worship, fasting, and prayer. When the infant Jesus was brought to the temple, she gave thanks to God and spoke about Him to people awaiting Jerusalem’s redemption.

Her ministry directed attention toward the Messiah.

Yet Scripture preserves no extended speech from Anna. Luke tells us what her testimony concerned without recording her precise words.

Anna’s place in the story is brief, but her contribution matters. At the beginning of Jesus’ earthly life, she helped others recognize God’s saving work.

Her example reminds us that prophetic significance cannot be measured by the number of pages a prophet leaves behind. A faithful witness may accomplish God’s purpose without becoming a biblical author.

Philip’s four daughters make the distinction even clearer.

During Paul’s journey through Caesarea, he stayed in the home of Philip the evangelist. Luke briefly mentions that Philip had four unmarried daughters who prophesied.

He records none of their messages. He does not even give their names.

Still, their prophetic activity belongs to the biblical account of the church’s life. They were not members of the Twelve, and no biblical books are attributed to them. The Spirit’s work extended beyond the circle of people whose writings became Scripture.

Together with Anna, these women also demonstrate that God’s distribution of the prophetic gift included women. Whatever other questions Christians debate about ministry and church office, the biblical recognition of women who prophesied is explicit.

There is a limit to what we can say about Philip’s daughters. Since Luke does not tell us what they prophesied, we should not supply messages for them. The prophet Agabus appears shortly afterward in the narrative, but his recorded prediction cannot simply be attributed to them.

The evidence is modest and sufficient: these women exercised prophecy, and their prophetic messages were not preserved in Scripture.

What follows from these examples?

First, the prophetic gift and the production of biblical books are distinguishable. God’s work through prophets was broader than the writings collected in the canon.

Second, prophecy could serve immediate spiritual needs. Encouraging a struggling congregation, calling people to faithfulness, or directing attention toward Christ could be a meaningful prophetic task.

Third, the absence of a prophet’s writings from the Bible does not, by itself, establish that the prophet was false.

These conclusions help clarify the discussion of modern prophecy. They do not settle it.

The existence of Anna or Philip’s daughters does not prove that a particular person today speaks for God. Nor does it, by itself, resolve the debate between Christians who believe prophecy continues and those who believe it ceased after the apostolic period.

It establishes a narrower point: **“This person did not write Scripture” is not a sufficient reason to reject a claim to prophetic ministry.**

A modern claim still requires examination on its own merits.

This brings us to the relationship between prophetic authority and biblical authority.

If God gives a genuine message, that message deserves a faithful response. Calling a ministry noncanonical should never become a convenient way of saying that God’s guidance can be ignored.

At the same time, a claimed revelation does not gain authority merely because its speaker invokes God’s name. The claim must be tested.

Within Seventh-day Adventist theology, Scripture provides the governing standard for that examination. A later prophetic ministry cannot legitimately overturn biblical teaching, establish a competing gospel, or place itself beyond correction by the written Word.

This is how Adventists understand the relationship between the Bible and Ellen White.

Ellen White reported receiving visions and dreams beginning in 1844. Over the following decades, her ministry addressed Christian living, biblical themes, education, health, mission, and the life of the developing Adventist Church.

The church recognizes her ministry as an expression of the prophetic gift. Its statement of belief also affirms that the Bible remains the standard by which all teaching and experience must be tested.

The examples of noncanonical prophets provide a biblical category within which Adventists understand that claim. A person may exercise a genuine prophetic ministry without adding another book to the Bible.

But the category cannot do the work of authentication.

The reasoning cannot simply be: “Anna was a noncanonical prophet; therefore Ellen White was a prophet.” Many people could fit the description of someone claiming revelation outside Scripture. Their claims would still need to be examined.

A responsible evaluation of Ellen White must consider her teachings, the historical evidence surrounding her ministry, her witness concerning Jesus, and the fruit of her work. Difficult questions deserve careful answers, including attention to context, literary practices, and the distinction between what she claimed and what later readers have claimed for her.

Respect for a prophetic ministry should encourage honest investigation.

The same principle applies to Adventist interpretations of Revelation. Adventists connect its language about the “testimony of Jesus” and the “spirit of prophecy” with the continuing prophetic gift. They understand that gift as having a place in the life of God’s people near the end of history.

Other Christians interpret these expressions differently, including as references to the church’s witness about Jesus. Revelation does not name Ellen White. Connecting its language to her ministry involves theological interpretation and further historical judgment.

Acknowledging this does not require abandoning the Adventist position. It requires stating the argument with appropriate care.

How, then, should ordinary believers approach claims of prophecy?

Paul gives a concise principle:

> “Prove all things; hold fast that which is good.”
>
> — 1 Thessalonians 5:21, KJV

Here, “prove” means to examine or test. In the surrounding passage, Paul cautions believers against suppressing the Spirit and treating prophetic messages with contempt. His instruction joins openness with discernment.

Christians should therefore examine what a claimed message teaches, how it relates to Scripture, and what kind of life and community it encourages. Does it faithfully present Jesus Christ? Does it call people toward repentance, love, truthfulness, and obedience? Can its claims withstand honest scrutiny?

These questions apply to modern prophetic claims generally, including those made within our own religious communities. Familiarity and denominational loyalty cannot replace discernment.

For Adventists, this also has practical consequences for reading Ellen White. If the Bible is the supreme standard, believers need to study it directly and carefully. Quoting a later writer cannot replace understanding a biblical passage in its literary and historical setting.

Recognizing prophetic authority should deepen biblical engagement. It should cultivate humility, responsibility, and a willingness to examine evidence.

Anna’s brief appearance offers a fitting picture of the purpose of prophetic ministry. When she recognized Jesus, she gave thanks and spoke about Him. Her witness directed people toward the One in whom their hope would be fulfilled.

Judas and Silas strengthened believers. Philip’s daughters exercised a gift whose messages we no longer possess. Their ministries remind us that God’s work is not measured only by what becomes a biblical book.

For Chukwuma Theology, the challenge is to hold these truths together: Scripture remains the church’s governing standard, prophetic claims require testing, and the Holy Spirit’s freedom cannot be reduced to our assumptions about whom God may use.

A faithful prophetic ministry helps people hear God’s Word more clearly, follow Jesus more closely, and serve others more faithfully. That is a purpose worth understanding—and a claim worth examining with care.
