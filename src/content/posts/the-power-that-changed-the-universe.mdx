The most powerful moment I have ever witnessed in a delivery room did not look like power at all.

There was no shouting. No dramatic intervention. A patient was bleeding, the numbers on the monitor were sliding in the wrong direction, and every instinct in the room wanted someone to *do something* — fast, visible, decisive. What actually saved her was slower than that. It was a team that stayed still enough to think, a resident who spoke calmly instead of loudly, a hand held while we waited for blood to arrive. To anyone watching from the hallway, it would not have registered as strength. It would have looked like waiting.

Corinth would not have recognized that room as powerful at all.

---

## Corinth Worshipped at the Altar of Impressiveness

Corinth was one of the wealthiest cities in the Roman Empire, rebuilt from ruin into a showcase of ambition, and it ran on a single currency: visibility. Rhetoric was theater. Philosophers gathered followers the way a modern platform gathers followers — through polish, confidence, and the sheer performance of intelligence. To be powerful in Corinth was to be watched, quoted, and admired.

The church absorbed this without noticing. Believers split into fan clubs around their favorite teachers — "I follow Paul," "I follow Apollos," "I follow Cephas" (1 Corinthians 1:12) — as though the gospel were a competition between personal brands. They ranked each other by spiritual gifts. They tolerated open immorality while boasting of their maturity. At the Lord's Table, the wealthy ate their fill while the poor went hungry, and somehow this coexisted with everyone's confidence that they were spiritually rich.

Paul does not treat these as separate problems requiring separate fixes. He treats them as one symptom of one disease: a congregation that had quietly swapped God's definition of power for Rome's.

That is not a first-century problem. It is a chronic condition. It shows up in any church, any era, any pastor's own heart, whenever influence starts to feel like the same thing as faithfulness.

## The Cross Was God's Rebuttal

Paul's argument does not open gently.

<div class="scripture">
  "For the message of the cross is foolishness to those who are perishing, but to us who are being saved it is the power of God."
  <cite>1 Corinthians 1:18</cite>
</div>

The word behind "power" is *dynamis* — capacity, ability, the thing that actually gets something done, not the appearance of getting something done. Paul uses it again and again through this letter (1:18, 2:4, 4:20, 6:14, 15:43), circling the same claim from every angle: whatever *dynamis* actually is, Rome had it backwards, and so did Corinth.

The cross was Rome's tool for public humiliation. It announced, unmistakably, that the man hanging on it had lost. Paul looks at that same instrument and calls it the clearest display of divine power the universe has ever seen.

That claim only makes sense inside a bigger story. Scripture presents a controversy older than Corinth, older than Rome — a dispute over whether God's government can be trusted, whether His rule rests on love or on force. Ellen White traces this argument back to heaven itself, where the challenge was first raised against God's right to govern by anything other than compulsion. Calvary is where that argument gets settled. God does not answer the challenge to His authority by crushing His accusers. He answers it by dying for them.

That is not weakness wearing a disguise. It is the thing itself.

## The Spirit Does What Eloquence Cannot

Corinth prized brilliant speakers. Paul, by his own admission, was not one of them, and he refused to fake it.

<div class="scripture">
  "My speech and my preaching were not with persuasive words of human wisdom, but in demonstration of the Spirit and of power."
  <cite>1 Corinthians 2:4</cite>
</div>

I have sat through excellent arguments that changed nobody, and I have watched a plainly delivered truth land like a diagnosis nobody wanted but everybody needed. Information can be perfectly correct and still bounce off a closed heart. Only the Spirit gets underneath the resistance.

This is why Paul refuses to let the Corinthians separate their theology from their conduct. Spiritual gifts had made some of them impressive. It had not made them holy. His answer is not a new rule. It is a reminder of what had already happened to them: "You were washed, you were sanctified, you were justified in the name of the Lord Jesus Christ" (1 Corinthians 6:11). Power that only informs is not the power Paul is describing. Power that transforms — that is *dynamis*.

## The Government That Runs on Love

Then, in the middle of two chapters about spiritual gifts, Paul stops and says something none of them expected.

Prophecy, faith that moves mountains, knowledge that unlocks every mystery, even a body given up to be burned — worthless, he says, without love. It is easy to read 1 Corinthians 13 as a wedding reading and miss what it is actually doing in this letter. It is the hinge of Paul's whole argument about power. Every gift the Corinthians were ranking each other by is downstream of love, or it is nothing.

This is the deepest layer of the Great Controversy. Satan's kingdom has always run on fear, coercion, and self-preservation. God's kingdom runs on truth freely offered and love freely given. One government has to be propped up by force. The other one went to a cross rather than use any.

Love is not the soft alternative to power. Love is what power looks like when it belongs to God.

## Resurrection: Power Over the Last Enemy

First Corinthians ends where every honest theology of power eventually has to end: at the grave.

<div class="scripture">
  "Death is swallowed up in victory."
  <cite>1 Corinthians 15:54</cite>
</div>

Adventists read this chapter with a specific conviction that most of Christianity has quietly abandoned: human beings are not naturally immortal. We do not have a soul that slips free of the body at death and floats off to its reward. Immortality is a gift, promised for the resurrection, not a property we already possess (1 Corinthians 15:51-53). That distinction matters here, because it means death is not a doorway. It is an enemy — the last one, Paul says, to be destroyed.

Every other power we might name — political, financial, medical — eventually runs into that wall. I have practiced enough medicine to know exactly where my own power ends. Paul is not describing a God who manages death more skillfully than I can. He is describing a God who ends it. That is not an upgrade on human power. It is a different category of power entirely.

## What I Actually Reach For When I Want to Feel Powerful

I want to be honest about something here, because this text will not let me hide behind a lectern.

I did not become a physician because I was drawn to weakness. I was drawn to the opposite — to being the person in the room who knows what to do, who is not afraid, who can be trusted with the worst moment of someone else's life and bring them out the other side. Most days, that instinct serves my patients well. But I know exactly what it feels like when it curdles. It is the quiet satisfaction of being needed. It is the relief of a room that runs smoothly because I ran it. It is, if I am not careful, the same thing Corinth wanted from its philosophers — to be watched, to be impressive, to be indispensable.

The same temptation follows me to church. An elder can prepare a lesson to be admired instead of to be true. A leader can measure a ministry's health by attendance and applause instead of by whether anyone in the room is becoming more like Christ. I have caught myself doing both. Paul is not writing to the Corinthians as though he stands outside their temptation, correcting it from a safe distance. He is writing as a man who once persecuted the church in the name of his own righteousness, and who now says the only boast he has left is in a cross that made a fool of every credential he used to be proud of (1 Corinthians 1:31; Galatians 6:14). I recognize that man. I am still learning to become him.

## What a Powerful Church Actually Looks Like

Paul never lets the theology float free of practice. If the cross really is the standard, a few things follow.

A church shaped by this text will value Christlikeness over charisma, because gifts without love are noise (1 Corinthians 13:1). It will let the Spirit do the converting, because eloquence never saved anyone (1 Corinthians 2:4-5). It will treat holiness as evidence of grace already at work, not as a hoop to jump through for God's approval (1 Corinthians 6:11). It will hold the resurrection, not comfort or control, as its actual hope (1 Corinthians 15:19).

None of that will look impressive from the hallway. It rarely does.

The strongest thing that has ever happened in the history of the universe looked, for three days, like a defeat. A crucified God is not a contradiction the gospel has to explain away. It is the whole argument. Not control. Not conquest. Not the loudest voice in the room. Love, poured out and left on a cross, turned out to be the one power strong enough to empty a grave.

That is the power Paul wants reproduced in us. Not the power to win. The power to love like that.
