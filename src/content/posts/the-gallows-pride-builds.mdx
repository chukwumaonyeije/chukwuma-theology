Haman had almost everything a man in ancient Persia could want.

He had wealth, influence, position, sons, servants, and access to the king. When he entered a room, people bowed. When he spoke, the machinery of an empire moved. By every visible measure, Haman was winning.

Then one man refused to stand.

Mordecai remained seated at the king's gate, and suddenly Haman could no longer enjoy any of it. He gathered his wife and friends, recited the inventory of his greatness, and then revealed the diagnosis:

<div class="scripture">
  "Yet all this availeth me nothing, so long as I see Mordecai the Jew sitting at the king's gate."
  <cite>Esther 5:13, KJV</cite>
</div>

That may be one of the most accurate pictures of pride in all of Scripture.

Pride does not merely want something. Pride wants **everything arranged around the self**. It whispers that I must be recognized, vindicated, admired, respected, and—perhaps most dangerously—right. It can hold a kingdom in one hand and still feel impoverished because one person will not bow.

Pride is not satisfied by abundance. It is tormented by whatever it cannot control.

## When Everything Is Still Not Enough

In his August 29 sermon at Atlanta North Seventh-day Adventist Church, Pastor Evan Knott located Haman's pride inside a much larger biblical story. Pride did not begin in Persia. Haman is not an isolated villain who appeared without ancestry. His spirit reaches backward to the first rebellion in heaven and forward into every human heart.

Lucifer looked at the place God had given him and desired a throne that was not his. "I will ascend into heaven," he said. "I will exalt my throne above the stars of God... I will be like the most High" (Isaiah 14:13–14). In Eden, the serpent offered humanity the same ambition in a different form: "Ye shall be as gods" (Genesis 3:5).

The temptation in both scenes is not simply to possess more. It is to reject the limits of being a creature. What God has given is treated as insufficient because the self has imagined a higher place.

Haman inherits that logic. Honor from an empire is not enough if Mordecai withholds his. Prosperity is not enough if someone else possesses dignity Haman cannot revoke. So his wife and friends recommend a solution proportionate to his wounded ego: build a gallows fifty cubits high—roughly seventy-five feet—and ask the king to hang Mordecai upon it.

This was not merely an execution. It was theater. A gallows that high was meant to turn another man's destruction into a public monument to Haman's importance.

That is what pride eventually builds: structures designed to make the self look taller by making someone else disappear.

## The God Who Is Present in the Silence

Then the story turns on something almost embarrassingly ordinary.

The king cannot sleep.

He calls for the royal chronicles to be read. Mordecai's forgotten act of loyalty is discovered. The king asks how Mordecai was rewarded and learns that nothing was done. At precisely that moment, Haman enters the court expecting permission to execute Mordecai.

Instead, Haman is commanded to place the king's robe on Mordecai, set him on the royal horse, and lead him through the streets proclaiming the honor of the man he hates.

The timing is exquisite. It is also theological.

God's name is famously absent from the Book of Esther. There is no burning bush, no pillar of fire, no prophet announcing, "Thus saith the LORD." Yet God's providence is everywhere. It appears in a sleepless night, an opened record, a forgotten service remembered, and an enemy arriving at exactly the wrong—or providentially right—moment.

The apparent silence of God is not the absence of God.

We often expect providence to announce itself with thunder. Esther teaches us to look again. Sometimes deliverance begins because a king cannot sleep. Sometimes God moves through decisions that appear routine, delays that feel accidental, and records someone happens to open at the necessary hour. What looks like coincidence from inside the moment becomes, in retrospect, the machinery of deliverance.

Haman came to the palace to arrange Mordecai's humiliation. He left holding the reins of Mordecai's horse.

And the reversal was only beginning.

## Evil Carries the Seed of Its Own Destruction

At Esther's second banquet, Haman's plot is exposed. The king orders his execution, and one of the attendants points toward the structure already standing at Haman's house:

<div class="scripture">
  "So they hanged Haman on the gallows that he had prepared for Mordecai. Then was the king's wrath pacified."
  <cite>Esther 7:10, KJV</cite>
</div>

The lesson is larger than poetic justice. As Pastor Knott summarized it, **evil ultimately destroys itself, while God remains sovereign over the timing and the outcome**.

This is deeply Adventist theology.

The Great Controversy is the biblical conflict between Christ and Satan over God's character, government, and right to rule. It reminds us that evil is real, devastating, and presently active—but it is not ultimately sovereign. God permits the controversy to unfold long enough for the character of both good and evil to become visible. He does not need to manufacture a false charge against evil. He allows rebellion to reveal what it has always contained within itself.

Destruction.

Sin promises enlargement and produces diminishment. It promises freedom and builds prisons. It promises a throne and constructs a gallows. Haman's end is not an arbitrary punishment disconnected from his choices. It is the mature fruit of the spirit he cultivated. The structure he designed to display his power becomes the place where the truth about that power is finally exposed.

This does not make evil less painful while it is unfolding. Esther and Mordecai faced a genuine death decree. God's sovereignty did not make the threat imaginary, and it does not make our present suffering trivial. The Great Controversy is not a doctrine for pretending that wounds do not hurt. It is the assurance that the wound does not get the final word.

Evil can be active without being sovereign.

That distinction is the difference between despair and hope.

## From Haman's Gallows to Christ's Cross

Pastor Knott then took the sermon somewhere deeper—from **Haman's gallows** to **Christ's cross**.

Haman built an instrument of death for an innocent man and eventually died upon it himself. Humanity also built an instrument of death through sin, violence, and pride. But at Calvary, the Innocent One willingly took the place of the guilty.

Christ is the exact opposite of Haman.

Haman grasped for greater status. Jesus "made himself of no reputation" (Philippians 2:7).

Haman demanded honor. Jesus accepted humiliation.

Haman sought another man's death to preserve himself. Jesus gave Himself to save His enemies.

Haman needed everyone beneath him. Jesus knelt to wash His disciples' feet.

The contrast is not incidental. It reveals the two opposing governments at the center of the Great Controversy. Satan's kingdom ascends by grasping, coercing, accusing, and destroying. Christ's kingdom descends in love. One builds a gallows for its enemies. The other accepts a cross for them.

<div class="scripture">
  "And being found in fashion as a man, he humbled himself, and became obedient unto death, even the death of the cross."
  <cite>Philippians 2:8, KJV</cite>
</div>

The cross therefore becomes the ultimate reversal. Satan appears to succeed in killing Christ, yet the very instrument intended to defeat Jesus becomes the means through which Satan's kingdom is judged. The accuser's strategy exposes the accuser's character. At Calvary, evil exhausts its argument in public—and resurrection morning reveals that love is stronger than death.

The gallows exposed Haman.

The cross exposed the enemy.

And the empty tomb announced the verdict.

## The Mordecai at My Gate

It is easy to identify pride when it is wearing Haman's robes. It is harder to recognize when it is wearing mine.

The question is not merely whether we can condemn Haman's pride. The question is whether we can diagnose our own.

Pride appears whenever being right becomes more important than being loving. Whenever someone else's success reduces our ability to appreciate the blessings already in our hands. Whenever recognition becomes necessary for contentment. Whenever an apology feels like annihilation. Whenever one Mordecai sitting stubbornly at the gate makes us forget everything God has already given.

The symptom may be anger, envy, defensiveness, or the quiet rehearsal of someone else's failure. But beneath it lies the same demand: *this person, this circumstance, this conversation must bow before me before I can have peace.*

That is a dangerous condition because pride rarely introduces itself honestly. It calls itself principle. It calls itself discernment. It calls itself protecting standards. Sometimes those concerns are legitimate. But a legitimate concern can still become a gallows when the hidden goal is not restoration but humiliation—not truth, but the satisfaction of seeing another person brought low.

So the heart requires a harder set of questions:

- Whose refusal to affirm me has become larger than all the ways God has blessed me?
- Whose success feels like a threat to my worth?
- In which argument have I confused winning with faithfulness?
- What private gallows am I building through resentment, accusation, or contempt?
- Would I rather see this person restored—or exposed?

The height of Haman's gallows revealed the depth of Haman's insecurity. Our structures may be less visible, but they rise from the same ground.

Pride always wants another seventy-five feet.

## The Way Down Is the Way Home

Jesus offers another way.

Humility. Surrender. Grace.

Humility is not pretending that truth does not matter or that evil should never be confronted. Esther confronted evil. Mordecai refused to bow. The gospel does not ask us to become passive in the presence of injustice. It asks us to surrender the need to exalt ourselves while confronting it.

Christian humility is not thinking less of the truth. It is refusing to use the truth as lumber for a gallows.

The Great Controversy may be cosmic, but its battlefield also runs directly through the human heart. Every day we choose between the way of Haman and the way of Christ—between grasping and giving, self-exaltation and self-emptying, the throne and the cross.

Haman reminds us where pride eventually leads.

Jesus shows us where humility leads.

One man climbed higher and higher until the height he built became the place of his ruin. The other descended lower and lower—into servanthood, humiliation, death, and the grave—until God raised Him above every name.

Pride builds gallows.

Love carries a cross.

And because the tomb is empty, we already know which way wins.
