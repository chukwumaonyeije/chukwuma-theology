Every physician learns to take a history before offering an opinion. A patient sits down, and before any treatment is discussed, you ask what hurts, since when, and how bad. Most patients are good at this. Give them an open-ended question and they will hand you a full chart of complaints, ranked and cross-referenced, sometimes with printouts.

The church has gotten very good at this too.

Ask almost anyone — inside the pews or well outside them — what is wrong with organized religion, and you will get a chart. The leadership is compromised. The doctrine is stale. The music is either too loud or too dead. Somebody said something insensitive in a business meeting eleven years ago and it has never been forgotten. Social media has turned this chart into a permanent, searchable record, updated in real time, cross-posted, and argued over in comment sections long after the original offense has been forgotten by everyone except the algorithm.

I am not writing to say the complaints are always wrong. Some of them are not. But I want to ask a physician's question about the chart itself: what are we actually treating here, and is criticism the treatment — or just a more sophisticated form of the disease?

Lesson 3 of this quarter's Sabbath School study puts the question more plainly than I would have dared. It anchors itself in Paul's appeal to a church that had every reason to fracture: "I appeal to you, brothers and sisters, in the name of our Lord Jesus Christ, that all of you agree and that there be no divisions among you" (1 Corinthians 1:10).

---

## A Church With a Chart of Its Own

Corinth was not an easy congregation. By the time Paul writes, the believers there have organized themselves into competing camps — some claiming Paul, some Apollos, some Cephas, some claiming Christ himself as though that were a fourth faction rather than the only one that mattered (1 Corinthians 1:12). They had a chart of complaints too, and it ran the same way ours does: personality preferences dressed up as principle, style disagreements dressed up as theology.

Paul does not respond by producing a better argument for his own faction. He goes underneath the argument entirely. Unity, he insists, is not the result of one camp winning. It is not achieved by organizational restructuring, a persuasive majority, or a sufficiently clever compromise. It is the fruit of believers surrendering themselves to Jesus Christ and letting the Holy Spirit do the work no policy can do.

This is worth sitting with, because it reorders the whole diagnostic process. Most of us assume unity is a governance problem — get the right structure, the right leadership, the right statement, and the fighting stops. Paul says the opposite. Unity is a heart problem before it is ever an org-chart problem. As each believer moves closer to Christ, they move, almost without noticing, closer to one another. You cannot legislate that closeness into existence. You can only be drawn into it, or not.

Policy can regulate behavior. Only the Spirit changes hearts.

---

## Unity Is Not Uniformity

The mistake most of us make next is assuming that if unity is not organizational, it must be personal sameness — that a healthy church is one where everybody thinks, prays, and serves identically. Paul spends all of 1 Corinthians 12 correcting exactly this.

The church, he says, is a *sōma* — a body, a single living organism with differentiated parts, each one doing something the others cannot. The hand is not the eye. The eye is not the foot. And critically, none of them is trying to be. A hand that spends its energy resenting the eye for not gripping things is not exhibiting unity. It is malfunctioning.

I think about this every time I am in an operating room with a team. The anesthesiologist is not doing my job, and I am grateful for that, because if I were doing hers we would both be doing it badly. Unity in that room does not mean everyone performs the same function. It means everyone is oriented to the same patient, under the same protocol, answering to the same attending. Diversity of function, under one Head — that is not a compromise of unity. That is what unity actually looks like when it is working.

Biblical unity does not flatten difference. It anchors difference to one Lord, one faith, one mission, and lets the difference do what it was designed to do.

---

## The Chart Says Mission

Here is where the lesson gets uncomfortable, because it does not stop at theory. It returns, again and again, to a plain, almost inconvenient fact: the church was organized for service and the salvation of souls. Not for internal debate. Not as a permanent committee for auditing itself. For mission.

Scroll through the comment section under almost any article about denominational politics — Adventist forums are no exception — and you will find believers who can produce, from memory, a detailed catalog of everything wrong with the institution, while investing very little measurable time in the actual work the institution exists to do. I do not say this with contempt, because I have been that commenter. It is far easier to diagnose an organization from the outside than to do the slow, unglamorous work of discipling one person toward Christ.

Jesus faced opposition through his entire ministry — from the religious establishment, from crowds who misunderstood him, from his own disciples on a bad day. He never let the controversy relocate his attention. He kept seeking and saving the lost while people argued about him.

Before I ask what is wrong with my church, the lesson insists I ask a different set of questions first. Am I praying for it? Am I serving in it? Am I helping one specific person come to know Christ? Am I contributing more solutions than complaints? Mission has a way of resetting perspective that argument never does. It is very hard to stay bitter about a board meeting while you are sitting with someone in the middle of their own diagnosis, watching them find hope for the first time.

---

## My Own Chart

I want to slow down here, because this is the part of the lesson I would rather skip.

It is far easier to run a differential on someone else's spiritual condition than my own. I can sit in an elders' meeting and, within minutes, identify exactly which committee is dysfunctional, which decision was political rather than pastoral, which member is more invested in being right than in being reconciled. I am good at this. It is, in a strange way, an extension of the same clinical instinct that serves me well at the hospital — pattern recognition, differential diagnosis, the ability to sort signal from noise quickly.

But Revelation 3 will not let me stop there. The message to Laodicea is addressed to a church that could not see its own condition — "wretched, miserable, poor, blind, and naked" — while insisting it needed nothing (Revelation 3:17). What unsettles me about that passage is not that it describes some other, more obviously compromised church. It is that Laodicea's defining symptom is the inability to self-diagnose. That should terrify anyone who is confident in their own chart.

Revival, the lesson insists, does not begin with institutional reform. It begins with personal repentance. It begins with a prayer I do not enjoy praying: *Lord, show me my own heart.* Not the church's heart. Not the committee's heart. Mine. When I have actually meant that prayer — and I can count the times — the Spirit has not handed me an argument to win. He has handed me humility, and patience, and a noticeable drop in my appetite for being the smartest critic in the room. Those are not abstractions. They are the specific, textured fruit of a believer who has stopped auditing the church long enough to let the church's Head examine him instead.

I do not think I am unusual in this. I think most of us are far more comfortable identifying the church's wretchedness than confronting our own.

---

## What the Pioneers Had Left

The early Adventist believers understood something about this that I find myself returning to. After October 22, 1844, they had lost their timeline. The date had come and gone, and the movement that had organized itself around a specific hour now had nothing to show for its confidence except disappointment, public ridicule, and each other.

They did not disperse. They gathered — for prayer, for the patient re-searching of Scripture, for the slow work of seeking God's guidance without the comfort of certainty. Their unity was not built on having the right answer. It could not have been; they had just discovered they did not have it. Their unity was built on a shared, stubborn commitment to Christ and to His Word, held together in the room, in the silence, in the waiting.

Ellen White, reflecting on what actually holds believers together, put it this way: "The secret of unity is found in the equality of believers in Christ." Not sameness of opinion. Not organizational conformity. Equality at the foot of the cross — which is the one place none of us gets to outrank anyone else.

---

## The Prescription

The church will not be perfect until Christ returns. It is, and will remain until then, a gathering of redeemed sinners still growing into the people they are becoming. That is not a defect in the design. It is the design.

But Scripture calls us to something sturdier than criticism.

Pray more than you critique. Serve more than you complain. Speak the truth, and speak it in love, which is a harder discipline than speaking it at all. Guard the unity of the Spirit the way you would guard a patient's vital signs — not because the numbers are perfect, but because the life depends on them.

I keep coming back to the last line of the lesson, because I did not write it and I cannot improve on it: the closer we come to Christ, the closer we come to one another.

That is not sentiment. It is diagnosis.

And it is, finally, the only chart worth keeping.
