*The New Tower of Babel — Part Two of a Two-Part Series*

**What if the biblical answer to the dangers of artificial intelligence is not less technological innovation, but a fundamentally different theology of why we innovate?**

In [the first article of this series](/chukwuma-theology/posts/technology-is-not-divinity-the-new-tower-of-babel/), we examined the Tower of Babel, the emergence of technological messianism and the danger of attributing divine qualities to human inventions and their creators.

But Christianity offers more than a warning against technological pride.

It also offers a positive vision of human creativity, scientific discovery and technological stewardship.

To understand that vision, we must turn from the plains of Shinar to the wilderness of Sinai.

## Bezalel: The Divinely Inspired Craftsman

In Exodus 31, God introduces Moses to a man named Bezalel, who would oversee aspects of the construction of the sanctuary.

God describes his abilities in remarkable language:

> “And I have filled him with the Spirit of God, in wisdom, in understanding, in knowledge, and in all manner of workmanship.”
>
> — Exodus 31:3, NKJV

Bezalel was a craftsman, designer and technical innovator.

His abilities were not opposed to spirituality. They were expressions of divine gifting directed toward a sacred purpose.

This presents a compelling alternative to the Tower of Babel.

At Babel, technological ingenuity was directed toward human self-exaltation.

In the construction of the sanctuary, human ingenuity was directed toward service to God.

Both narratives involve sophisticated craftsmanship, coordinated human effort and ambitious construction.

What distinguishes them is their underlying purpose.

| The theology of Babel | The theology of Bezalel |
|---|---|
| Self-exaltation | Service |
| Human autonomy as ultimate | Dependence upon God |
| Power as an end | Power as a responsibility |
| Innovation for personal glory | Innovation for human flourishing |
| Knowledge as a means of domination | Knowledge as a gift to be shared |
| Technology as salvation | Technology as an instrument |

The distinction is not between technology and faith.

It is between technology as an instrument of self-glorification and technology as an instrument of stewardship.

## Human Creativity and the Image of God

Genesis 1:27 teaches that humanity was created in the image of God.

Although the theological meaning of the *Imago Dei* extends far beyond intellectual or creative ability, our capacity to imagine, design and create can be understood as one expression of our relationship with a creative God.

Scientific discovery and technological innovation need not threaten Christian faith.

They can represent the responsible exercise of the intellectual gifts entrusted to humanity.

As a physician and technology enthusiast, I have witnessed how human ingenuity can alleviate suffering, expand access to knowledge and improve the quality of human life.

Artificial intelligence offers extraordinary opportunities to continue this work.

The Christian question is not whether these capabilities should exist, but how they should be exercised and toward what ends they should be directed.

## Technological Messianism and the Promise of Salvation

Christianity makes a fundamental distinction between improving the human condition and redeeming the human condition.

Medicine can treat disease. Engineering can improve living conditions. Artificial intelligence can expand access to knowledge.

These are worthy endeavors.

But they are not equivalent to salvation.

The Christian understanding of humanity's fundamental problem is not merely a lack of intelligence, information, resources or computational power.

It is the problem of sin.

A more intelligent civilization is not necessarily a more righteous civilization.

A wealthier civilization is not necessarily a more compassionate civilization.

A technologically advanced civilization is not necessarily a morally mature civilization.

Artificial intelligence may help humanity accomplish its objectives more efficiently, but efficiency does not establish whether those objectives are morally desirable.

If our objectives are compassionate, technology may amplify compassion. If our objectives are exploitative, technology may amplify exploitation.

The fundamental moral question therefore precedes the technological question.

What kind of people are we becoming?

Technology can help us live longer.

Christ offers eternal life.

Technology can expand our access to information.

Christ calls us into a relationship with the One who is truth.

Technology can help us understand aspects of the universe.

It cannot reconcile humanity to God.

These distinctions are essential if Christians are to engage with artificial intelligence without adopting a secular theology of salvation.

## The Great Controversy and the Desire for Divine Status

From the perspective of Seventh-day Adventist theology, this discussion intersects with the overarching narrative of the Great Controversy.

The conflict between good and evil is fundamentally concerned with the character of God, the nature of His government and the relationship between created beings and their Creator.

The biblical language associated with rebellion against God is revealing:

> “I will ascend above the heights of the clouds, I will be like the Most High.”
>
> — Isaiah 14:14, NKJV

Although Isaiah 14 directly addresses the king of Babylon, Adventist theology has traditionally understood its language as also illuminating the deeper spiritual rebellion associated with Lucifer.

The temptation presented to humanity in Genesis 3 echoes this ambition:

> “You will be like God.”
>
> — Genesis 3:5, NKJV

The temptation was not simply to acquire knowledge. It was to acquire knowledge in a manner that promised independence from God and His authority.

Ellen G. White develops this understanding of self-exaltation and rebellion in *Patriarchs and Prophets*, particularly in the chapters “Why Was Sin Permitted?” and “The Tower of Babel.”

Within this theological framework, the desire to occupy God's position is not a new temptation introduced by artificial intelligence.

It is an ancient temptation expressed through new technological possibilities.

The instruments change.

The underlying spiritual struggle remains.

## Wealth, Power and Biblical Accountability

The growing influence of technology billionaires also raises an important question: Should extraordinary wealth and technical capability exempt individuals from the standards applied to ordinary citizens?

Scripture suggests precisely the opposite.

James warns against partiality based on wealth and social status:

> “My brethren, do not hold the faith of our Lord Jesus Christ, the Lord of glory, with partiality.”
>
> — James 2:1, NKJV

Jesus articulates an even more demanding principle:

> “For everyone to whom much is given, from him much will be required.”
>
> — Luke 12:48, NKJV

Greater capability does not imply diminished accountability.

Greater capability implies greater responsibility.

The Christian response need not be resentment toward successful entrepreneurs. Wealth creation and technological innovation can contribute meaningfully to human flourishing.

But neither financial success nor technological achievement should place anyone beyond legitimate ethical scrutiny.

The biblical standard is not that extraordinary individuals deserve fewer obligations.

It is that extraordinary gifts carry extraordinary responsibilities.

## Five Principles for Christian Technological Stewardship

How, then, should Christians approach artificial intelligence?

I would propose five foundational principles.

**1. Creativity without self-deification.**

We should celebrate human ingenuity while remembering that our intellectual capabilities are gifts, not evidence of our divinity.

**2. Innovation without idolatry.**

We should embrace useful technologies without allowing them to become sources of ultimate meaning, identity or security.

**3. Knowledge without moral arrogance.**

Access to vast amounts of information does not necessarily produce wisdom. Intelligence must remain accountable to ethical principles.

**4. Power without exemption from responsibility.**

Those who develop and control powerful technologies should recognize the obligations that accompany their influence.

**5. Progress without confusing it with salvation.**

We should work enthusiastically to alleviate suffering and improve human life while recognizing that technological advancement cannot replace the redemptive work of Christ.

These principles allow Christians to participate constructively in technological civilization without surrendering their theological convictions.

## Conclusion: Build Like Bezalel, Not Like Babel

The Christian response to artificial intelligence should be neither uncritical enthusiasm nor reflexive opposition.

It should be stewardship.

We can develop powerful systems without worshiping them.

We can admire technological visionaries without surrendering our moral judgment to them.

We can pursue scientific discovery without assuming that science will eventually eliminate our need for God.

We can celebrate human creativity precisely because we recognize that humanity was created in the image of a creative God.

The builders of Babel and the craftsman Bezalel represent two fundamentally different orientations toward human ingenuity.

One seeks to make a name for humanity.

The other places human capability in the service of God.

As we enter an era of increasingly powerful artificial intelligence, we must decide which of these theological models will shape our relationship with technology.

Let us build.

Let us innovate.

Let us discover.

But let us build like Bezalel, not like Babel.

Technology may be the closest thing we have to magic. But its creators are not gods.

And the salvation of humanity will not emerge from a data center.
