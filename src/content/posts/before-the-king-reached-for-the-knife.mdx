*A Sabbath School reflection on Jeremiah 36:3*

I wonder what would have happened if, after the first three columns, the king had asked Jehudi to read them again.

It was winter. A fire burned in front of King Jehoiakim while Jehudi read aloud the words Jeremiah had dictated to his scribe, Baruch. Every three or four columns, the king cut away the portion he had heard and fed it to the fire. Three men in the room asked him to stop. He kept going until the whole scroll was ash (Jeremiah 36:21–25).

It is the knife I remember from this story. But preparing for this week’s Sabbath School lesson, *Now Hear This!*, I found myself several verses back, before Baruch ever set pen to scroll. In verse 3, God tells Jeremiah what he hopes will happen when somebody reads this scroll aloud.

## What God wanted the hearer to do

There are three movements in that verse. The people of Judah may **hear** what is coming. Each may **turn** from the evil he is doing. And the Lord may **forgive**. Notice who acts first. While Judah is still going about its business, God calls for a scroll. He takes the trouble to get a warning into the hands of people who have given him ample reason to give up on them.

As a physician, I recognize something of that purpose in the phone call that follows a troubling result. The finding may be frightening to say aloud. You make the call because there is still something the person on the other end can do. In Jeremiah the stakes are moral. God names what Judah has done, and he tells them himself why he wants them to hear it. He desires to forgive the very people whose conduct the scroll records.

Then the scale of the verse changes. God addresses a nation, but the turning happens one person at a time. In Jeremiah 22:13–17, the prophet speaks against a king who builds with cedar and makes his neighbor work without wages. Imagine hearing that passage as the man who laid the boards. What would the king’s repentance look like from your end of the road? At the very least, somebody from the palace would show up with your pay.

## Whose name comes to mind?

In an Adventist Sabbath School class, we could spend a good hour on the authority of the prophetic word. We believe the Spirit gives the church the gift of prophecy, and we recognize that gift in the ministry of Ellen White. In *Prophets and Kings*, she reads this very scene in light of God’s concern for Judah’s good (chapter 35, p. 435). That puts a searching question to those of us who take prophetic counsel seriously. When I bring a passage to someone else’s attention, what good do I hope will come of it *for that person*? Would they recognize that desire in the way I speak to them?

The question gets harder when I am the one holding the lesson. I can picture myself asking a class why Jehoiakim refused correction, listening carefully to the answers, and driving home satisfied that we understood the text. Meanwhile, someone may still be living with a careless thing I said in front of other people. I can hear the sentence I would like to offer. “I hope you know what I meant.” It asks the person I embarrassed to do one more thing for me. A more honest beginning would be, “What I said about you was unfair. May I go back to the people who heard it and correct it?”

That is where I feel the mercy in verse 3. I can tell the truth about the harm because forgiveness was already in God’s heart when he brought it to my attention. And the person who bore the harm has a place in the story of my repentance.

After the fire, God tells Jeremiah to take another scroll. Baruch writes the words again, and more besides. The judgment on Jehoiakim stands. Yet the words the king tried to destroy have traveled all the way to a Sabbath School class where you and I can still hear them (Jeremiah 36:27–32).

This Sabbath, when the reading is over, whose name will you carry home? What might that person hear from you before the day is out?
