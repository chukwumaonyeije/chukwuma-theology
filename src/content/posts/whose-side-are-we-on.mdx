The fireworks were louder this year, and so was everything underneath them.

America turned two hundred and fifty on the fourth of July, 2026 — the Semiquincentennial, the official commemorations called it, America250. It should have been a season of unclouded celebration. Instead I watched it arrive in a country that argues about what it is even celebrating. Cookouts where certain subjects got quietly fenced off before the burgers came off the grill. Neighbors who fly different flags in the same cul-de-sac and mean two different countries by them. Christians — my own brothers and sisters in Christ — absolutely certain that Jesus, if He showed up in the flesh this week, would be standing at their rally, wearing their colors, nodding along to their grievances.

We do this so easily we rarely notice we are doing it. We do not ask whether we are on God's side. We ask God to get on ours.

Scripture has already dealt with a man who made that exact mistake, standing in the dark outside a fortified city, sword in hand, absolutely sure the question in front of him was tactical.

## A General Asks a Soldier's Question

Joshua is standing near Jericho. The wall is in front of him, ancient and enormous, and behind that wall is a nation that will not surrender without a fight. He lifts his eyes and sees a man standing there with a drawn sword. Not sheathed. Drawn — ready.

Joshua does what any competent commander would do. He asks the only question that matters on a battlefield:

<div class="scripture">
  "Art thou for us, or for our adversaries?"
  <cite>Joshua 5:13, KJV</cite>
</div>

It is a completely reasonable question. It is also, as it turns out, the wrong one.

<div class="scripture">
  "Nay; but as captain of the host of the LORD am I now come. And Joshua fell on his face to the earth, and did worship, and said unto him, What saith my lord unto his servant?"
  <cite>Joshua 5:14, KJV</cite>
</div>

Read that "Nay" slowly. It is not a hedge. It is not diplomatic ambiguity, the way a general might decline to commit to either faction in a civil war he'd rather not join. It is a flat refusal of the premise underneath the question. Joshua wanted to know which side this warrior would fight on. The warrior's answer dismantles the entire category. He has not come to be recruited into Israel's war. He has come as its Commander.

That distinction — between an ally you enlist and a Lord you obey — is the whole sermon, and I think it is one of the most theologically loaded corrections in the Old Testament.

## No Common Angel

Before we get to what the Commander says, we need to settle who He is, because it changes everything about how Joshua's worship should strike us.

Adventist reading of this text, going back generations, has identified the "captain of the host of the LORD" not as a created angel but as a **Christophany** — a pre-incarnate appearance of the Son of God Himself. Two details in the text make that identification almost unavoidable.

First, He accepts worship. When Joshua falls on his face, the Commander does not stop him, correct him, or redirect the gesture toward God the way faithful angels do every single time Scripture records the same scene. John tries this twice in Revelation — falls at an angel's feet — and both times gets the same firm correction: "See thou do it not... worship God" (Revelation 19:10; 22:8–9). Angels who love God refuse worship instinctively, immediately, without exception. This Commander does not refuse it. He receives it.

Second, He declares the ground holy. "Loose thy shoe from off thy foot; for the place whereon thou standest is holy" (Joshua 5:15) is not a new command — it is a quotation, deliberately echoing what God said to Moses at the burning bush (Exodus 3:5). Ordinary ground does not become holy because a created being happens to be standing on it. Ground becomes holy because the uncreated God is present on it. Ellen White read the scene the same way: "This was no common angel... It was the Son of God" (*Patriarchs and Prophets*, 491–492).

So before Joshua hears a single word of battle strategy, he is standing in the presence of Christ.

<div class="callout callout-insight">

**Word Study: Joshua 5:13–15**

**שַׂר** (*sar* — "captain") — a word used throughout the Old Testament for princes, chiefs, and commanders of every rank. Here it belongs to no earthly army. This is the Prince, full stop.

**צָבָא** (*tsava* — "host") — the same root behind one of the Bible's great titles for God, *YHWH Tsevaot*, the LORD of Hosts. It names the mustered armies of heaven — every angel, every power, all of it under a single command.

**קֹדֶשׁ** (*qodesh* — "holy") — set apart, exclusively claimed. Holiness in Hebrew is not primarily about moral purity; it is about ownership. This ground belongs to God alone, and Joshua's sandals — the ordinary tools of a man walking his own path — have no business on it.

**נָשַׁל** (*nashal* — "loose, draw off") — the verb behind "loose thy shoe." It describes a stripping away, a removal of what is common so that what remains can meet the holy unshielded.

</div>

Christ has not come to Joshua as a strategist. He has come as a King, and the first thing a king requires is not a battle plan. It is allegiance.

## The Question Behind Every Flag We Fly

Here is why this text will not leave me alone this particular July.

Joshua's question — *are you for us or against us* — is not a first-millennium-BC artifact. It is the operating question of nearly every religious nationalism that has ever existed, ancient or modern. It is the question behind every war fought with God's name stitched onto a banner. It is the question behind every political movement, right or left, that has ever quietly promoted its platform to Scripture and started calling its opponents the adversaries of righteousness.

And it is a question the Commander of heaven's armies refuses to answer on our terms, every single time it is asked, because it is built on a category error. God is not a combatant available for recruitment into human factions. He does not have a side in the way a voting bloc has a side. He is sovereign *over* every human conflict, which means He cannot be reduced to a participant *in* one without ceasing, in that moment, to be who He actually is.

This is where the Great Controversy framework does real work, because it reframes the whole question. The conflict that actually matters — the one Scripture says will decide everything — is not Democrat against Republican, nationalist against globalist, or any other configuration of tribes we invent for ourselves. It is Christ against the accuser, played out across the whole of human history, and every earthly battle we fight is, at best, a shadow cast by that larger war. When we drag God into our tribal skirmishes, we are not elevating our cause. We are shrinking Him — trying to fold the Commander of the universe into the dimensions of a bumper sticker.

And here Adventists should feel this more sharply than most, not less. We are a people whose entire prophetic reading of Revelation warns us about what happens when religious authority and political power fuse into a single throne. We have spent generations defending liberty of conscience — the principle, carried in publications like *Liberty* magazine, that the state has no business enforcing anyone's religious observance and the church has no business borrowing the state's sword. That conviction did not come from nowhere. It came from watching what happens, across history, when someone convinces themselves that God has endorsed their flag. The moment we start assuming Christ has joined our side, we have already taken the first step toward the very fusion of church and crown our own eschatology warns us against.

So Joshua's question is not naive. It is dangerous, in exactly the way it feels innocent. And the Commander's correction is not a technicality. It is a mercy — an interruption before the mistake could compound.

## What I Have Wanted God to Endorse

I want to be honest about how familiar this temptation is to me personally, because I do not preach this from outside it.

I came to this country as a four-year-old, brought by parents who wanted something better than what they were leaving. I grew up straddling two identities that were not always easy to hold together, when I was 14 I stood in a room, raised my hand, and became a citizen — an oath I took seriously, a country I love without reservation. And precisely because I love it, I have had to watch myself do the thing this text warns against. I have caught myself, in an argument about policy or justice or the direction of the nation, quietly assuming that my read of the situation was not just my opinion but God's verdict. That the position I already held was the position Christ obviously held too, if He were only paying close enough attention.

That is not conviction. That is Joshua's question, dressed in modern clothes. *Art thou for us, or for our adversaries?* Which is really just another way of asking, *Lord, will You please ratify what I have already decided?*

The Commander's answer to me is the same answer He gave Joshua. Not "yes." Not "no." A correction of the whole premise. He did not come to bless my side of the argument. He came to ask whether I am on His.

That question costs something to sit with honestly, because the honest answer is not always yes. There have been seasons when my political convictions were louder in me than my discipleship — when I was more certain about a policy than I was surrendered to the Prince who stood before Joshua with a drawn sword. Feeling certain about a cause is not the same as being aligned with Christ. I have had to learn, slowly and more than once, that the two can point in opposite directions, and that only one of them is holy ground.

## Worship Before Instructions

Notice what the Commander does not do once Joshua falls at His feet. He does not immediately hand over the battle plan for Jericho. He does not sketch the strategy, deploy the troops, or explain the trumpets and the silence and the seven-day march. All of that comes later, in the next chapter.

First, He gives one instruction only: take off your shoes. This ground is holy.

Worship comes before strategy. Surrender comes before the plan is ever revealed. This is the pattern across the whole of Scripture — Moses at the bush, Isaiah crying "woe is me" before he is sent, Ezekiel falling on his face, Daniel losing his strength in the presence of glory, Peter begging Jesus to depart from him, John dropping "as dead" before the risen Christ in Revelation 1:17. Every genuine encounter with the living God produces humility before it produces service. Nobody in Scripture gets the mission before they get the posture.

And the posture is not complicated. It costs Joshua nothing but his sandals and his assumptions. He does not need a better argument. He does not need a stronger claim on God's allegiance. He needs to remove what is common — his own plans, his own leverage, his own sense of who is running this campaign — so that what remains can stand, unshielded, in front of the holy.

This is where grace and liberty actually meet, and it is not where we usually go looking for them. We tend to think of liberty as the freedom to hold our own position and defend it. Scripture offers a stranger, better liberty: freedom from the exhausting work of needing God on our side at all. Grace means we do not have to win the argument to be safe. We do not have to secure heaven's endorsement for our politics before we can rest. The Commander already stands over every outcome, already reigns over every election and every empire, already holds the field regardless of who we voted for or which side we were sure was right. That sovereignty is not a threat to our freedom. It is the only thing that actually frees us from carrying an argument we were never built to referee.

## Loose Your Shoe

So here is where I want to land this, on the far side of a holiday that celebrates a nation I love, standing in front of a text that will not let any nation, including this one, stand where only Christ belongs.

Stop asking whether God is on your side. Ask, honestly and often, whether you are on His. Those are not two versions of the same question. One keeps you at the center, deciding what is right and inviting God to co-sign it. The other puts Him at the center and asks you to realign.

That realignment is not passive. The Commander did not tell Joshua to stand down. He told him to take off his shoes and then, in the very next chapter, gave him a mission that would require everything he had. Surrender to Christ's leadership does not exempt us from caring about hunger, poverty, or the abuse of power — it corrects the spirit in which we address them. It trades partisan urgency for holy urgency. It trades the need to defeat an opponent for the call to love a neighbor, especially the neighbor whose sign says something different than yours.

Take off your sandals. Put down your certainty. The ground in front of you is holy, and it was never yours to claim for a side.

The most powerful position in this universe was never a political one. It is the one Joshua found himself in, face down, sandals off, in front of a Commander who had already won.
