*“Thus far” is not a museum caption. It is permission to take the next step without a map of the whole road—and without letting the next fear erase the last mercy.*

*This follows [Thus Far: The Theology of Ebenezer](/chukwuma-theology/posts/thus-far-the-theology-of-ebenezer/), written after a family retreat vespers on 1 Samuel 7:12.*

There is a moment after the stone is raised that Scripture does not romanticize.

Samuel names the place. Israel can finally say out loud what fear had been trying to drown out: **Thus far the Lord has helped us.** Then the people have to walk home. The Philistines are still a people. The children still wake at night. Tomorrow still arrives with its ordinary demands and its capacity to surprise.

Remembrance is not the same thing as arrival.

In the earlier essay I argued that faith needs witnesses—memorial stones that interrupt fear’s better bookkeeping. The danger on the other side of that argument is quieter. We can treat Ebenezer like a finished exhibit: polished, labeled, safely behind glass. We visit it when we need inspiration. We quote it when we need a closing line. Then we return to living as though the next mile must be secured by competence alone.

But “thus far” was never meant to end the journey. It was meant to steady the next step.

## The open country after the memorial

Most of the Christian life is lived between stones.

Behind us stands a mercy we can name—if we are honest enough to keep the story. Ahead of us stands something we cannot yet name: another diagnosis, another board decision, another child becoming someone we do not fully recognize, another prayer that has outlasted our preferred timetable.

Between those places is open country. No thunder. No Jordan wall of water held back for the photo. Just ordinary faithfulness under incomplete information.

That is where many of us lose the plot.

We remember the crisis and forget the help. Or we remember the help and demand that God reproduce it on command, as though providence were a subscription we had already paid for. Or—most common among capable people—we remember both, then quietly conclude that the next stretch is ours to manage without needing to ask again.

Ebenezer will not sponsor that conclusion.

The stone says God helped. It does not say God retired.

## What faith does after “thus far”

If the first essay asked families to name where God helped them, this one asks a harder follow-up: **what will you do with the memory when the next fear begins its argument?**

Faith after Ebenezer is not optimism. Optimism edits the future until it feels manageable. Faith carries a true past into an unfinished future.

It does at least three things.

**It refuses amnesia.** When the next Philistine line appears, faith does not pretend the last deliverance was a fluke, a personality strength, or a coincidence with good timing. It brings the prior mercy into the present tense as evidence about God’s character—not as a guarantee of identical outcomes.

**It refuses superstition.** The stone is a witness, not a charm. Israel once treated the ark like a lucky object and learned that sacred things cannot be used to control God. We can make the same mistake with testimony. “God helped us then” is not a spell we recite to force His hand now. It is a confession that frees us to ask again without shame.

**It refuses despair’s monopoly on realism.** Realism is not the exclusive property of the worst-case scenario. In a universe where Christ has died and risen, and where Adventists still look for His return, the unfinished story is not proof that help has ended. It is proof that we are still inside a conflict larger than our private timelines—and that the final word has not yet been spoken.

Between the stones, faith practices a simple, costly motion: look back long enough to tell the truth, then step forward without requiring a full itinerary.

## Hope that does not erase grief

Forward-looking language can become a kind of pastoral violence if it is used to hurry people past their wounds.

“Thus far” already made room for empty chairs. The follow-up must keep that room open. Some families will name an Ebenezer and still go to bed with the same unanswered prayer. Some will remember a mercy and still feel the absence it did not remove.

Christian hope is not the erasure of grief. It is the refusal to let grief become the only historian of our lives.

The resurrection does not ask us to call death a minor inconvenience. It announces that death will not keep the archive forever. Until then, we are allowed to lament what is unfinished while we steward what has already been given.

That is why the next step after Ebenezer is often smaller than people expect.

Not a strategic five-year plan for spiritual resilience.  
An apology without a defense.  
A Sabbath kept when the inbox is still loud.  
A sentence spoken at family worship: *I am frightened, and I still believe God has helped us.*  
A decision to stop revising the story until God becomes a footnote and we become the heroes.

These are acts of hope with dirt under their nails.

## Living toward the next help

If Sabbath can serve as a weekly Ebenezer—time in which we remember whose we are—then the days after Sabbath are the open country. We return to clinics, classrooms, kitchens, and committee tables. We carry unfinished work into a week that will not wait for our feelings to catch up.

The question is not whether another stone will eventually be needed. In a fallen world, another memorial will almost certainly be required. The question is whether we will arrive at that future need having practiced trust in the meantime—or having practiced the quieter unbelief of self-sufficiency.

In the great controversy, accusation works in both directions. It tells us God cannot be trusted because the next threat is real. It also tells us we scarcely needed God because the last threat is past. The life between the stones answers both lies the same way Samuel answered Israel: by keeping help in the public record, and by refusing to walk forward as orphans.

So here is the practice I am learning to keep after the retreat:

When fear opens its ledger, open yours.  
Name the last mercy without polishing it.  
Ask for the next help without bargaining.  
Take the next faithful step without waiting to feel finished.

You do not need to see the whole road.  
You need to remember who brought you to this mile marker.

The God who helped us thus far has not exhausted Himself getting us here.  
Between the stones, that is enough to walk on.

Thus far.  
And then the next step.
