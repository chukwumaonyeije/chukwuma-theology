*The New Tower of Babel — Part One of a Two-Part Series*

**What happens when a civilization that increasingly questions the existence of God begins to attribute divine qualities to the people who create its technology?**

We may be witnessing the emergence of a new form of an ancient spiritual temptation.

## The Search for a Modern Messiah

I recently listened to a provocative podcast discussion about the growing influence of technology billionaires and the public's frustration with their expanding authority over society.

One statement particularly captured my attention:

> “Technology is the closest thing we have to magic.”

The speaker suggested that as traditional religious participation declines, society increasingly looks toward technological visionaries to fulfill something resembling a messianic role.

Steve Jobs once occupied aspects of this cultural position. Today, figures such as Elon Musk and Jensen Huang have become prominent symbols of humanity's technological ambitions.

We look to these individuals not merely to provide us with better products, but increasingly to explain the future, solve our social problems and even redefine what it means to be human.

As someone who genuinely appreciates technology and actively participates in the development and application of artificial intelligence, I find this phenomenon both fascinating and theologically troubling.

There is nothing inherently wrong with celebrating human ingenuity. But there is something profoundly dangerous about confusing technological genius with divine authority.

## When Technology Appears Miraculous

The British science-fiction writer Arthur C. Clarke famously articulated what has become known as Clarke's Third Law:

> “Any sufficiently advanced technology is indistinguishable from magic.”

Imagine presenting a modern smartphone to someone living in the twelfth century. With this small object, one could communicate across oceans, summon vast repositories of knowledge, translate unfamiliar languages and observe events occurring thousands of miles away.

To a medieval observer, these capabilities might appear supernatural.

Artificial intelligence has introduced another dimension to this phenomenon. We can converse with machines, generate computer programs through ordinary language, analyze scientific literature and accelerate mathematical discovery.

Even those of us who use these technologies regularly occasionally find ourselves astonished by their capabilities.

Yet there is an important distinction between something appearing miraculous and something actually being divine.

Our inability to comprehend a process does not make that process supernatural. Nor does the ability to develop an extraordinary technology confer supernatural wisdom upon its creator.

**The danger begins when our admiration for the invention becomes reverence for the inventor.**

## The Tower of Babel: Humanity's Ancient Technological Ambition

The biblical account of the Tower of Babel provides a remarkably relevant framework for examining our contemporary relationship with technology.

Genesis 11:3–4 records:

> “Then they said to one another, ‘Come, let us make bricks and bake them thoroughly.’ They had brick for stone, and they had asphalt for mortar.
>
> And they said, ‘Come, let us build ourselves a city, and a tower whose top is in the heavens; let us make a name for ourselves, lest we be scattered abroad over the face of the whole earth.’”
>
> — Genesis 11:3–4, NKJV

We often concentrate on the tower itself, but notice how the narrative begins.

It begins with a technological innovation.

The biblical writer specifically mentions the manufacture of bricks and the use of asphalt as mortar. These technologies enabled the builders to undertake an ambitious construction project.

Technology expanded the possibilities of human civilization.

But the theological problem was not the manufacture of bricks. It was not engineering, architecture or urban development.

The problem was the purpose to which these capabilities were directed.

Notice the language:

- Let us build ourselves a city.
- Let us construct a tower reaching into the heavens.
- Let us make a name for ourselves.

The emphasis is on collective human greatness, security and self-exaltation.

The Tower of Babel represents humanity's attempt to establish its own security, significance and destiny independently of God.

The bricks were not sinful.

The mortar was not sinful.

The engineering was not sinful.

The fundamental problem was the orientation of the human heart.

## Are We Building Another Tower of Babel?

The parallels between the ancient narrative and contemporary technological civilization are striking.

The builders of Babel possessed bricks and mortar. We possess semiconductors, neural networks, enormous data centers and increasingly sophisticated algorithms.

Their technological ambition was expressed through architecture. Ours is expressed through computational power.

Some technological futurists envision a civilization in which artificial intelligence will eventually overcome many of the fundamental limitations of human existence.

We hear predictions about the elimination of disease, the extension of human life, the abolition of material scarcity and even the possibility of transferring human consciousness into digital systems.

Some of these ambitions represent legitimate scientific aspirations. Others venture into highly speculative territory.

But collectively, they raise an important theological question:

**At what point does the pursuit of technological progress become an attempt to achieve salvation through human ingenuity?**

| Ancient Babel | Contemporary civilization |
|---|---|
| Bricks and mortar | Semiconductors and algorithms |
| Architectural innovation | Artificial intelligence |
| A tower reaching heaven | The pursuit of superintelligence |
| Making a name for ourselves | Wealth, influence and legacy |
| Security through human achievement | Confidence in technological solutions |

These parallels do not mean that artificial intelligence is inherently sinful.

Rather, Babel offers a theological warning about the relationship between human capability and human pride.

## The Deification of the Technology Entrepreneur

Human beings possess an enduring desire for figures who can explain what we cannot understand, alleviate our anxieties and provide solutions to apparently insurmountable problems.

Technology entrepreneurs can become particularly attractive candidates for this role.

They produce extraordinary innovations. They accumulate enormous wealth. They speak confidently about the future. Their companies increasingly control infrastructure upon which modern civilization depends.

The sociologist Max Weber described a related phenomenon as *charismatic authority* — authority derived from the perceived exceptional qualities of an individual.

In technological culture, this authority can gradually expand beyond its original domain.

A person who demonstrates exceptional competence in engineering or business may subsequently be regarded as an authority on morality, education, public policy and the future of civilization.

But technical competence and moral wisdom are not interchangeable.

A brilliant engineer is not necessarily a brilliant ethicist. A successful entrepreneur is not necessarily a wise political philosopher. An individual capable of generating billions of dollars is not necessarily capable of explaining the meaning of human existence.

The ability to create something extraordinary does not confer extraordinary moral authority.

## Idolatry Without Statues

When Christians discuss idolatry, we often imagine ancient civilizations bowing before carved images of wood or stone.

However, the biblical understanding of idolatry is considerably broader.

Paul writes:

> “Who exchanged the truth of God for the lie, and worshiped and served the creature rather than the Creator.”
>
> — Romans 1:25, NKJV

The essential problem of idolatry is the elevation of something created into a position that properly belongs to God.

An idol need not be made of gold.

**It can be made of silicon.**

Artificial intelligence becomes a potential object of idolatry when we expect it to provide ultimate answers to questions that transcend its proper domain.

The irony is particularly striking.

Human beings create machines using their God-given intellectual capabilities. They then risk looking toward those same machines as sources of ultimate wisdom and authority.

The prophet Isaiah exposed a similar contradiction in Isaiah 44:14–17, describing a man who uses part of a tree to warm himself and prepare his food, while fashioning the remainder into an object of worship.

The absurdity is evident when the material is wood.

It becomes less immediately apparent when the material is silicon and the object can engage us in sophisticated conversation.

Nevertheless, the underlying theological danger remains remarkably similar.

## The Bricks Have Changed, but the Temptation Remains

The Tower of Babel is not merely an ancient story about a failed construction project. It is a recurring narrative about the human condition.

Every generation develops new instruments through which humanity expresses its intelligence, creativity and ambition.

Our technological capabilities have changed dramatically.

Our susceptibility to pride, self-exaltation and idolatry has not.

Technology may be the closest thing we have to magic.

But its creators are not gods.

### Coming in Part Two: From Babel to Bezalel

Does the biblical warning against technological pride mean that Christians should be suspicious of artificial intelligence, scientific discovery and technological innovation?

Not necessarily.

There is another remarkable biblical account of technological ingenuity — one in which God Himself empowers a skilled craftsman to create something extraordinary.

His name was Bezalel.

The contrast between Babel and Bezalel may provide one of the most compelling biblical frameworks for developing a Christian theology of artificial intelligence.

The question is not whether we should build.

The question is whom we are building for.
