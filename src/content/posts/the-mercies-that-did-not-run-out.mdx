I want to begin with a confession.

I have quoted Lamentations 3:22–23 many times. At bedsides. In prayer. In the margins of clinical notes I wrote for patients who were not going to get the news they were hoping for. I have said the words — *"the steadfast love of the LORD never ceases, his mercies never come to an end"* — and meant them. But I am not sure I always understood what I was saying.

Because the text is not from a quiet morning. It is not from a journal entry written in peace. It is from the ruins of Jerusalem, 586 BCE, written by a man who watched everything burn.

And I think that matters for how we receive it.

---

## Before We Get to the Promise, We Have to Sit in the Poem

Lamentations is an acrostic. Five chapters. Each of the first four organized around the 22 letters of the Hebrew alphabet. Chapter 3 — where our text lives — gives each letter three verses. Sixty-six verses of walking from *aleph* to *taw*.

The scholars who ask why the poet chose this form offer a compelling answer: the acrostic imposes order on chaos. When everything has collapsed, the writer reaches for the alphabet. When you cannot control what happened to your city, your temple, your people, your future — you can at least say A, B, C. You can hold the form even when you cannot hold anything else.

The form is itself a theological argument. Before Jeremiah says a word about mercy, he says: there is still structure here. There is still something that holds.

We should not rush past that. The stability the poet trusts is not the stability of his circumstances. Jerusalem is still rubble. The Babylonians are still there. The exiles are still gone. The structure that holds is underneath all of that, not instead of it.

---

## The Word the Translation Doesn't Quite Capture

Verse 22 opens with the Hebrew word *chesed*. Most English versions render it "steadfast love" or "lovingkindness" or "mercy." All of those are correct. None of them is fully adequate.

*Chesed* is covenantal. It is the love of a God who has bound Himself to a people by oath. And here is what makes this word so heavy in its context: Israel had broken the covenant. The exile was not an accident. The prophets had been warning about it for generations. The judgment that fell on Jerusalem was real judgment.

And yet Jeremiah does not say: we are not consumed because we were good enough. He says: we are not consumed because of *chesed*. Because of the covenant-loyalty of a God who, even when every condition of the agreement has been violated, does not walk away from what He has promised.

We should be finished. We are not finished. That is not our achievement.

Ellen White, in *Patriarchs and Prophets*, writes about this pattern across Israel's history. The God who kept calling to a people who kept walking away. The God who remained faithful across generation after generation of apostasy — not because they deserved it, but because His character does not change based on our performance. "The mercy and love of God did not cease to be manifested toward them."

That is the *chesed* of Lamentations 3:22. Not a feeling. A fact.

---

## New Every Morning

Verse 23 gives us one of the most stunning claims in all of Scripture: His compassions — *rachamim* in Hebrew, from the root for womb — are new every morning.

The Hebrew word for new here is *chadashim*. Same root as the new moon. Same root as the new thing God announces through Isaiah. Same root as the new covenant. These are not recycled mercies. They are not yesterday's grace repackaged for today. They are genuinely, substantively new.

I think about this in clinical terms. In medicine, we talk about reserve — physiological reserve, cardiac reserve, pulmonary reserve. The capacity a system has left when it is under stress. What Jeremiah is describing is a God whose mercy has no reserve problem. It is not a finite tank being drawn down. Every morning, full. Every morning, renewed. Not because of anything that happened yesterday. New.

This is why the Adventist understanding of the Sabbath is not incidental to this theology — it is central to it. The Sabbath is a weekly monument to a God who creates and re-creates. Who builds and restores. The pattern of the week is the pattern of divine generativity. Six days the world is sustained. On the seventh, we stop and remember the character of the One who sustains it. We remember that the mercies are not running out. We remember that the morning comes because God sends it.

Ellen White connects this explicitly in *Patriarchs and Prophets*: "The Sabbath was given to all. It was made for man." Not for convenience. Not for religious ceremony. For man. As a sign that the covenant God has not abandoned the creature He made.

---

## The Interior Turn: What This Costs Me to Believe

I need to spend more time here.

I have sat with women who received diagnoses that could not be fixed. I have explained to parents that the baby they were expecting would not survive. I have spoken careful, honest words in the quiet of ultrasound rooms where the news arriving was not what anyone had prayed for.

In those rooms, I have believed in the *chesed* of God. And I have also felt the weight of what believing that requires. It does not make the diagnosis untrue. It does not produce a different ultrasound image. It does not explain why this family, this morning, this baby.

Lamentations does not give me those answers either. The poet in the ruins does not receive a theological explanation for why Babylon came. He does not get a word of exculpation from God. He gets something different. He gets a ground to stand on while the questions remain open.

The mercy is not an answer to the suffering. It is a presence within it.

I think about the Adventist understanding of the Great Controversy — the cosmic conflict between Christ and Satan that runs beneath human history and surfaces in moments of crisis. Ellen White's framing in *The Great Controversy* is not that God is absent from suffering. It is that He is fully present within it, working through it, refusing to coerce but constantly reaching toward us. The suffering is real. The enemy is real. And the mercy of God is also real, and it does not come to an end.

That is a much harder thing to hold than the throw-pillow version of this verse. It does not resolve the tension. It says: the tension is real, and I am here in it with you, and My mercy is still operative.

I find that harder to receive. And I find it more true.

---

## Great Is Your Faithfulness

The passage closes with a declaration that has become the title of one of Christianity's most beloved hymns. "Great is your faithfulness." *Emunatecha* in Hebrew. From the root *aman* — the origin of our word amen.

When we say amen at the end of a prayer, we are using the same root. We are saying: this is solid. This can be trusted. This is load-bearing.

The faithfulness of God — *emunat YHWH* — is Jeremiah's final claim from the ruins. Not that things look good. Not that the future is clear. Not that the present pain has been explained. But that the One who made the covenant is faithful to it. That the character of God has not shifted because the temple burned. That the morning comes not because of our merit but because of His nature.

The morning is an argument.

Every sunrise is a data point in the case for the faithfulness of God. Not proof by coercion — God does not coerce faith. But evidence available to anyone willing to look. The fact that there is a morning. The fact that the mercies are still there when you wake up. The fact that you woke up.

Seventh-day Adventists believe we are living in a specific moment in prophetic history — the antitypical Day of Atonement, the closing work of the investigative judgment, the final preparation before the Second Coming. The stakes in this moment are high. The pressure on the people of God is real. Ellen White's description in *The Great Controversy* of what lies ahead is not comfortable reading.

And into that context, Lamentations 3:22–23 speaks with particular force. Because what Jeremiah needed in the ruins of Jerusalem is the same thing we need in the closing scenes of earth's history: not an escape from the pressure, but a ground beneath it. Not the absence of the storm, but the knowledge that the One who controls the morning is faithful.

---

## What To Do With This

A few specific things. Not abstractions.

**Read Lamentations.** Not just verses 22 and 23. The whole book. Sit in the grief before you arrive at the declaration. The declaration means more when you have earned it by traveling through what precedes it.

**Interrogate your morning.** Not your morning routine. Your orientation in the morning. Do you wake reaching for your phone or reaching for the Word? The poet's "new every morning" is not passive — he is turning his attention (verse 21) toward the *chesed* of God. That turn is a choice. Make it deliberately.

**Notice where you are trying to find a ground that is not God.** Adventists are not immune to this. We can be very busy with the machinery of denominational religion — committees, programs, evangelistic series, administrative structure — and still be groundless. The *chesed* of Lamentations 3 is not a church program. It is a character. It is the character of the God you meet in private, in Scripture, in prayer.

**Hold the promise honestly.** Do not tell people in pain that everything is fine because God is faithful. That is not what Jeremiah said. Jeremiah said God is faithful while standing in a city that was not fine. The faithful God is present in the not-fine. Let the promise hold what it actually holds, not more and not less.

---

The ruins have not outlasted the mercy.

The morning still comes.

Great is His faithfulness.
