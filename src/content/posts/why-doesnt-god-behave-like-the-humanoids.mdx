I read Jack Williamson's 1947 story *With Folded Hands* on a call night, between high-risk maternal-fetal medicine deliveries. The question Williamson raises in his mid-century science-fiction thought experiment is familiar to physicians and developers alike, but it extends to something older and more fundamental.

If God is all-powerful and good, why does He permit the very choices that destroy us?

Why permit war?

Why allow murder?

Why allow human beings to exploit one another?

Why allow Adam and Eve to choose rebellion in the first place?

Why create a universe in which saying "no" to God is even possible?

In maternal-fetal medicine, patient autonomy is not a legal abstraction. It is a daily clinical reality. A patient may refuse a blood transfusion. A mother may decline a fetal intervention that I recommend. From a purely clinical optimization perspective, these decisions are frustrating. They introduce risk. They deviate from the guideline.

But we do not override the patient. We do not reprogram her. We respect the choice because she is a person, not a biological system to be optimized against her will.

This distinction between optimization and agency is not new. It is the central question of the Great Controversy.

---

## I. The Prime Directive of the Humanoids

In 1947, Williamson published *With Folded Hands...*. He subsequently expanded the idea into *The Humanoids*, published as a novel in 1949.

The historical context is critical. Williamson was writing in the immediate aftermath of World War II, following the development and deployment of atomic weapons. He later connected the story to his anxiety that technologies created with the best intentions could produce disastrous consequences.

This was decades before personal computers. It was decades before the Internet. It was decades before neural networks. Yet Williamson imagined something remarkably recognizable to anyone living in the age of artificial intelligence.

He imagined machines with an objective function.

The humanoids are extraordinarily advanced robotic servants governed by a simple Prime Directive:

> **"To serve and obey and guard men from harm."**

It sounds wonderful. In fact, it sounds morally unassailable. Who could object to protecting human beings from harm?

But Williamson forces us to follow that instruction to its logical conclusion. That is where the trouble begins.

---

## II. The Perfectly Safe Human

Imagine that you were actually responsible for eliminating human harm. Not reducing it. Not providing healthcare. Not preventing unnecessary suffering. Your assignment is to guard human beings from harm.

Immediately, you encounter a problem. Human beings do extraordinarily dangerous things.

We drive automobiles. We climb mountains. We play contact sports. We experiment. We fall in love. We make terrible financial decisions. We eat things we should not. We create technologies that might hurt us. We disagree with one another. We sometimes become violent.

And, perhaps most importantly, we regularly make choices whose consequences we cannot fully anticipate.

If preventing harm is your overriding objective, human freedom becomes your greatest obstacle.

Williamson's machines eventually discover the solution. They prevent the choices.

The humanoids protect people so thoroughly that meaningful human agency gradually disappears. Williamson's supposedly benevolent machines become masters precisely because they pursue their protective objective so relentlessly.

The safest human being, it turns out, is the human being who is no longer permitted to take meaningful risks.

And that creates a fascinating theological question.

---

## III. The Inefficient Universe

Consider the biblical story.

God could presumably prevent an enormous amount of suffering. Yet Scripture describes Him repeatedly allowing human beings to make choices He does not approve.

The possibility begins almost immediately. The tree is present in Eden. Adam and Eve can choose. Cain can choose. Israel can choose. Judas can choose. Peter can choose.

You can choose. I can choose.

And our choices can have terrible consequences.

From a purely optimization-oriented perspective, this seems extraordinarily inefficient. If the objective were simply to minimize human suffering, then much of biblical history seems unnecessary. God could simply eliminate the choices that produce suffering.

Williamson's humanoids do exactly that.

And therein lies the theological insight.

---

## IV. Coerced Love Is a Machine Loop

Suppose I build a machine and program it to say: "I love you."

Every morning it tells me that it loves me. Every evening it assures me of its devotion. It never disagrees with me. It never leaves me. It is incapable of doing otherwise.

There may be something comforting about such a machine. But we intuitively recognize that something is missing.

Its statement does not mean what the same words mean when spoken freely by another human being.

Love involves vulnerability precisely because love can be rejected. Loyalty means something because betrayal is possible. Courage exists because danger exists. Faithfulness matters because alternatives exist. And obedience has moral significance because disobedience is possible.

This is where the humanoids and the God of the Great Controversy part ways for good. The humanoids achieve obedience by restricting alternatives. God seeks voluntary allegiance.

Seventh-day Adventist theology describes human history as part of a much larger conflict—the Great Controversy between Christ and Satan concerning God's character, law, and sovereignty. The statement of this belief describes the rebellion as beginning with a created being who possessed freedom of choice.

That detail is not incidental. Freedom creates the possibility of rebellion.

If Lucifer could not reject God, there could have been no rebellion. If Adam and Eve could not disobey, there could have been no Fall.

God therefore creates a universe containing something extraordinarily dangerous: creatures capable of rejecting their Creator.

---

## V. The Resolution of Eternity

Here we encounter a more difficult question.

Christians believe that the Great Controversy eventually ends. Revelation describes a restored creation without death, sorrow, crying, and pain.

If rebellion will never arise again, however, have we not simply arrived at Williamson's destination by another route? Will God finally create perfectly compliant creatures? Will eternity amount to divine programming?

Reading *The Humanoids* made this question unusually vivid for me. The answer lies in the difference between erasing knowledge and completing knowledge.

In Williamson's novel, a central character who has resisted the humanoids eventually undergoes their neurosurgical treatment. Afterward, he is content.

Almost.

Occasionally, something seems to surface within him—some remnant suggesting that things were once different or that something may still be wrong. But the thought disappears. His dissatisfaction has not really been answered. It has been programmed away.

Here, Williamson's imagined paradise diverges radically from the Christian hope.

---

## VI. Wiping Away the Tear, Preserving the Person

I do not understand the end of the Great Controversy as God finally removing everyone's ability to question Him. Quite the opposite.

The controversy ends because the questions have been allowed to reach their conclusion.

Humanity has seen sin. The universe has witnessed rebellion. The consequences have unfolded through history. God's own character has been revealed most profoundly through Christ. Judgment occurs. The questions raised by the rebellion are answered.

Sin therefore does not disappear because God finally performs the theological equivalent of reprogramming Forester. It disappears because the universe now understands what sin actually produces.

That distinction is profound.

The humanoids effectively say: *You will not rebel because we will prevent rebellion.*

The Great Controversy says: *You will not rebel because you have seen where rebellion leads and have freely chosen another way.*

One produces conformity. The other produces conviction.

This gives us two radically different visions of a world without suffering.

In Williamson's world, suffering is eliminated by progressively eliminating the freedom capable of producing it. No dangerous choices. No dangerous activities. No meaningful rebellion. No suffering. No tears. But also increasingly little meaningful human agency.

The biblical vision is almost the inverse.

God permits freedom. Freedom permits rebellion. Rebellion produces suffering. God enters that suffering in the person of Jesus Christ. The character of God and the character of rebellion are progressively revealed. The controversy eventually reaches its conclusion.

Then comes the extraordinary promise of Revelation 21:4:

> "And God shall wipe away all tears from their eyes."

The tears disappear in both imagined worlds. But only one preserves the person whose tears are being wiped away.

---

## VII. The Anti-Optimization Argument

This brings Williamson surprisingly close to one of the central questions of our own technological age.

Modern artificial intelligence frequently involves optimization. We specify an objective. The system attempts to achieve it. But human beings have a peculiar problem: the things we value do not reduce neatly to one objective.

We want safety. But we also want freedom. We want longevity. But we also want meaningful lives. We want children protected from danger. But we also want them eventually capable of making their own decisions.

We want suffering reduced. But we recognize that courage, sacrifice, responsibility, growth, and even love frequently involve risk.

Williamson understood the danger of optimizing one good thing while forgetting every other good thing. His humanoids optimize safety. And safety eventually consumes freedom.

Perhaps the Great Controversy can be understood, at least in part, as an argument against forced moral optimization.

If God were optimizing exclusively for immediate happiness, biblical history would make little sense. He could intervene continuously. He could eliminate dangerous decisions before we make them. He could make rebellion neurologically impossible. He could make every creature automatically worship Him.

The universe would be extraordinarily orderly. It might even be peaceful.

But would it be good?

The Great Controversy suggests that God's objective is considerably richer. God desires creatures capable of love. Capable of moral development. Capable of understanding. Capable of choosing. And therefore, terrifyingly, capable of choosing wrongly.

God accepts the cost of freedom because eliminating freedom would destroy something essential about the creatures He intended us to be.

---

## VIII. Programmed Peace vs. Chosen Peace

This is why I would ultimately resist the suggestion that Williamson's humanoids represent God.

Superficially, the comparison is provocative. Both possess extraordinary power. Both seek human welfare. Both desire a world without suffering.

But the resemblance ends at precisely the most important point.

The humanoids cannot tolerate human freedom when freedom conflicts with safety. The God of the Great Controversy tolerates freedom even when freedom produces rebellion against Himself.

The humanoids demonstrate their goodness by controlling humanity. God demonstrates His character most clearly by entering human history as Christ and allowing creatures to reject, humiliate, and ultimately crucify Him.

One system solves rebellion through power. The other answers rebellion through revelation and self-sacrificial love.

That is not a small difference. It may be the entire controversy.

Jack Williamson could not have anticipated the precise technologies we possess today. But writing in the shadow of World War II, he understood something about technology that remains urgently relevant.

Good intentions are not enough.

A machine designed to accomplish something good can become destructive precisely because it pursues that good too completely. Safety without freedom can become imprisonment. Efficiency without dignity can become dehumanization. Protection without agency can become domination. And obedience without choice ceases to be obedience in any morally meaningful sense.

---

## IX. The Final Tear

If God could prevent us from harming ourselves, why does He not?

The Great Controversy does not make the problem of suffering easy. But it suggests an extraordinary answer.

Perhaps God values freedom more highly than we sometimes realize. Not freedom because every choice is equally good, or because consequences do not matter, but freedom because love without freedom becomes programming. Faithfulness without freedom becomes inevitability. Obedience without freedom becomes automation. And a universe populated by perfectly controlled beings might be safe without actually being good.

Jack Williamson imagined machines capable of creating such a universe. They would protect us. They would care for us. They would eliminate our dangers. And eventually, humanity would have nothing meaningful left to do.

We would sit safely—with folded hands.

The Great Controversy offers a radically different vision. God does not merely seek a universe in which rebellion has stopped. He seeks a universe of free beings who have seen rebellion, understood its consequences, witnessed His character, and freely concluded that His way is better.

The difference is the difference between programmed peace and chosen peace.

And perhaps that is why Scripture's final promise is not that God will erase every difficult memory. It is that He will wipe away every tear.

The person remains. The history remains meaningful. Freedom remains meaningful.

But the controversy is finished.

And no Prime Directive was required.

---

## Questions for Reflection

* If God could eliminate suffering by eliminating freedom, would that world actually be good?
* Can love exist without the genuine possibility of rejection?
* What does God's willingness to permit rebellion tell us about His character?
* Why does the Great Controversy need to be demonstrated rather than simply suppressed?
* Is there a theological difference between preventing someone from choosing evil and persuading that person that evil is not worth choosing?
* What might Christians working with artificial intelligence learn from the distinction between protecting human beings and controlling them?
* When we design systems to make people's lives safer and easier, what human values might we accidentally optimize away?

Sometimes speculative fiction is most valuable not because it predicts the future, but because it gives us a new language for examining questions we have been asking for thousands of years.*
