In 1492, two Anglo-Irish families brought their war to the front steps of a cathedral.

The Fitzgeralds and the Butlers had been feuding over land, title, and influence for longer than anyone alive could remember. The Butler side lost a battle in the streets of Dublin and ran for the nearest sanctuary they could find: the chapter house of St. Patrick's Cathedral. They bolted the door behind them. Gerald Fitzgerald and his men stood outside it — armed, victorious, and unable to finish what they had started.

A door stood between two families who had spent a generation trying to destroy each other.

I keep thinking about that door. Not because the standoff is interesting, though it is. I keep thinking about it because of what happened next — and because Paul was writing about something structurally identical two thousand years earlier, to a church in Ephesus that had its own bolted door between two peoples who had spent a generation trying to destroy each other.

## The Wall Paul Is Actually Talking About

Ephesians 2:14 says that Christ himself is our peace, and that he has broken down "the middle wall of partition" between us.

<div class="scripture">
  "For he is our peace, who hath made both one, and hath broken down the middle wall of partition between us."
  <cite>Ephesians 2:14</cite>
</div>

The Greek is *mesotoichon tou phragmou* — the dividing wall of hostility — and Paul is not being poetic. He is describing an actual, physical, four-and-a-half-foot stone barrier that ran through the Jerusalem temple, the *soreg*, past which no Gentile was permitted to go on pain of death. Archaeologists have found the warning inscriptions. The threat was not rhetorical.

That wall was the architecture of an old world order: Jew and Gentile, insider and outsider, clean and unclean, us and them. Paul says Christ abolished it "in his flesh." Not around it. Not by negotiating a treaty at it. In his own body, on a Roman cross, he became the place where the wall came down.

Verse 16 uses a word that should stop a modern reader cold: *apokatallassō* — to reconcile, to restore to a former state of friendship after estrangement. Both parties, Jew and Gentile, are reconciled "unto God in one body by the cross," Paul says, "having slain the enmity thereby." The hostility itself is what dies. Not one side. Not the other. The hostility.

That is not how most reconciliation stories work. Most of them require a winner.

## Chancing an Arm

Which brings me back to the cathedral.

Fitzgerald stood outside the chapter house door with his men and his advantage, and he had a choice that every victor in every feud eventually faces: press it, or end it. He chose to end it. He ordered a hole cut through the heavy wooden door, and then he did something that had no strategic value at all. He pushed his own bare arm through the opening, into a room full of the family he had just defeated — unarmored, unprotected, entirely at their mercy.

James Butler could have taken that arm off at the elbow. Instead, he took Fitzgerald's hand.

That gesture is where the English idiom "chancing your arm" comes from, and I think it is one of the most theologically precise phrases in the language, because it names exactly what real reconciliation costs. It is not a treaty signed by lawyers on neutral ground. It is not a truce, where both sides keep their weapons and their suspicion and simply agree to stop shooting for a while. It is one party choosing to become vulnerable to the other, on purpose, with no guarantee of the outcome.

That is what the cross is. God did not negotiate peace with humanity from a position of safety. He chanced His arm. He hung it open on a Roman beam, in full view of everyone He had reason to distrust, and He let us decide what we would do with it.

That is also the deeper argument running underneath the whole Great Controversy. Satan's charge against God's government has always been that it cannot survive without force — that authority and vulnerability cannot coexist in the same being. Calvary is not God's tactical retreat from that charge. It is His answer to it. He did not win the argument by overpowering the accusation. He won it by opening His hand to the very people making it.

## The Diagnosis I Cannot Avoid

I want to sit here for a moment, because I think this is where the text is actually asking something of me, not just something interesting of me.

I know how to diagnose hostility. I have spent a career learning to read a chart for what is not written on it, to notice the small signs before the crisis, to name a pathology precisely enough that it can be treated. And I can tell you, with the same clinical confidence I would bring to a growth curve, that the dividing walls in front of me right now are not thinner than the one in the Jerusalem temple. In some ways they are more elaborate. In April 2026, Pew Research found that 93 percent of Republicans and 92 percent of Democrats now hold an unfavorable view of the opposing party — numbers that have become so normal we barely register them as remarkable anymore. I have watched that same hostility metastasize into families, into congregations, into Sabbath School classes where two members will not sit near each other because of something said about an election four years ago.

But I am not writing to diagnose the culture. That is the easy version of this essay, and I am tempted to stop there because it costs me nothing.

Here is the harder version. I have a bolted door of my own. There is a colleague whose call I let go to voicemail on purpose. There is a family member whose name I brace for on caller ID. In each case I have very good reasons — the kind of reasons that hold up in every conversation I have with myself. And in each case, if I am honest, what I actually want is not resolution. What I want is vindication. I want to be found right, and I want the other person to be the one who has to cut the hole in the door.

Ephesians 2 will not let me have that. Paul does not say Christ waited for the Gentiles to repent properly before he reconciled them. He does not say the wall came down because Jew and Gentile finally worked out their differences in mediation. He says Christ became our peace while the enmity was still fully alive, and he killed it himself, in his own body, before either side had done anything to deserve it. Grace runs ahead of resolution. It always has.

I do not enjoy that theology when it is aimed at me instead of at the culture.

## What the Door Still Says

Fitzgerald did not wait for Butler to prove he was trustworthy. He could not have. Trust is exactly what a feud destroys first. He had to act before the evidence was in, because the evidence was never going to arrive on its own. That is not naive. That is the only way a dividing wall has ever actually come down — in Dublin, in Ephesus, or in your kitchen this week.

The door of the chapter house at St. Patrick's Cathedral still exists. You can go see the hole in it. Six centuries later, it is still the most eloquent thing in the building — more eloquent than the stained glass — because it does not describe reconciliation. It enacts it. Paul says Christ "came and preached peace to you which were afar off, and to them that were nigh," and then, in verse 19, tells the Ephesian Gentiles they are no longer strangers and foreigners but fellow citizens with the saints, members of the household of God.

<div class="scripture">
  "Now therefore ye are no more strangers and foreigners, but fellow citizens with the saints, and of the household of God."
  <cite>Ephesians 2:19</cite>
</div>

Not tolerated. Not merely permitted through the gate. Family.

I do not think the practical application here is complicated, even though the obedience is. Find the door you have bolted. Find the person on the other side of it whose voicemail you let ring out. You do not need them to have earned it. You do not need to know how the room will receive your hand.

Cut the hole. Push the arm through anyway.

The wall is already down. We are just the ones still standing at the door, deciding whether to believe it.
