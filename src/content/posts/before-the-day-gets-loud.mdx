A Theological Reflection from Within the Seventh-day Adventist Tradition

> "Very early in the morning, while it was still dark, Jesus got up, left the house, and went off to a solitary place, where he prayed." — Mark 1:35

The night before, He had healed a man in the synagogue, driven out an unclean spirit, cured Simon’s fever, and then — as word spread through Capernaum — stood at the door until sundown receiving everyone the town brought to him. An entire population’s suffering, in a single evening. After that, Scripture says, He rose before dawn and went somewhere quiet.

I have thought about that a lot. Not the miracles. The morning.

## The Real Bible Study Problem

This week’s Sabbath School lesson is on how to study the Bible, and I want to resist the framing most of us bring to that conversation.

When Christians admit they struggle with Bible study, the confession usually sounds like this:

> “I don’t read enough. I’m not disciplined enough. I know I should do more.”

It is a guilt vocabulary. And guilt, while an accurate diagnosis of the symptom, is almost never the cure.

The deeper problem is not laziness or irreverence. Most believers I know genuinely respect Scripture. The deeper problem — and I suspect Ellen White identified it more precisely than anyone — is speed.

> “There is but little benefit derived from a hasty reading of the Scriptures. One may read the whole Bible through and yet fail to see its beauty or comprehend its deep and hidden meaning.” — Ellen White, *Steps to Christ*

The modern world is extraordinarily good at training us to hurry. We skim notifications, scan headlines, and swipe past content that doesn’t hook us within three seconds. Then we bring those same reflexes to the text that has never, in the history of human encounter with it, yielded anything of value to a person in a hurry.

**The deepest Bible study problem is not rebellion against Scripture. It is hurried familiarity with Scripture.**

## Time Is Not the Thing — Attention Is

The lesson this quarter draws on a cascade of texts that speak to the discipline of protected time with the Word. John 15 on abiding. Acts 17:11 on the Bereans examining Scripture daily. Jeremiah 29:13 on seeking with all the heart. The pattern is consistent: depth is not produced by occasional intensity. It is produced by repeatable contact.

What the lesson carefully distinguishes — and what I want to hold on to — is the difference between short and hurried. These are not the same thing.

A short Bible reading that is prayerful, attentive, and honest can be deeply nourishing. Ten minutes in Isaiah with an open posture is worth more than forty-five minutes of scanning commentary while your mind is still three conversations behind. The question is not how much time you are giving Scripture. The question is whether, when you arrive, you are actually there.

As a physician, my most consequential conversations rarely last more than a few minutes. What determines whether those minutes accomplish anything is not their length. It is whether the patient feels that I have set everything else aside and am actually present with them. Scripture requires the same quality of arrival.

## A Place to Not Be Somewhere Else

Mark 1:35 makes a detail out of what it does not have to mention: Jesus went to a solitary place. He could have prayed walking from one town to the next. He could have prayed in the synagogue or at the house. Instead, He withdrew deliberately to somewhere else.

There is practical wisdom here that is easy to dismiss as aesthetic preference. It is not. A recurring place reduces friction. When your body walks into the same corner of your home at the same early hour, something in the nervous system relaxes into the pattern. The first few minutes of prayer stop being occupied with settling down because settling down is already built into the ritual of arrival.

This matters most, in my experience, for the people who have the hardest time protecting quiet. If you have small children, aging parents, a chaotic work schedule, or a home that never quite goes silent — the question is not whether you can have a hermitage. The question is whether you can find a corner, a chair, a fifteen-minute window that is reliably yours before the day gets loud.

Jesus found his solitary place the morning after the hardest night in the Gospel’s early chapters. That is not incidental. The person who has nothing left in reserve is the person who most needs a quiet place to refill.

## Deep Study Is Not Reserved for Scholars

> “I meditate on Your precepts and contemplate Your ways.” — Psalm 119:15

> “Do not merely listen to the word, and so deceive yourselves. Do what it says.” — James 1:22

The lesson argues that genuine Bible study has a simple but non-negotiable structure: pray, read carefully, write, reflect, obey. None of these steps require a seminary degree. What they require is a willingness to slow down enough to actually encounter the text rather than just scan it.

Writing is the one that surprises people most. I have found — both as a physician who documents patient encounters and as someone who tries to sit with Scripture — that the act of putting something into words exposes what I actually understand versus what I only thought I understood. You can read a passage and feel conviction. Writing one sentence about what it means to you, specifically, in your actual life, will immediately reveal whether the conviction is intellectual recognition or genuine application.

Mark Finley’s counsel in the lesson’s supporting material: insert yourself into the biblical scene. Ask seriously — What is happening here? What is God doing? What is the person in this text seeing, feeling, being confronted with? What does the passage ask of me today? This is not scholarly work. It is engaged reading. It is the difference between watching a film and actually watching a film.

## The Blessing That Doubles When Shared

> “The Sovereign Lord has given me a well-instructed tongue, to know the word that sustains the weary. He wakens me morning by morning, wakens my ear to listen like one being instructed.” — Isaiah 50:4

The structure of this verse arrests me every time I read it: God teaches the prophet morning by morning, and what the prophet receives is not stored for private benefit. It becomes a word for the weary. The instruction is given so that it can be given again.

We treat Bible study as primarily private: my quiet time, my spiritual life, my formation. James 1 is more communal than that. The person who hears the Word and does it is described not as a solitary spiritual achiever but as someone whose religion expresses itself outward — toward orphans and widows, toward the vulnerable, toward anyone who needs someone to sustain them.

One reliable way to deepen what you study is to share it the same day. Not performing, not preaching — just telling another person the one thing that struck you. A text to a friend. A comment at the dinner table. A question in Sabbath School. The act of articulating what you received will show you exactly where understanding is clear and where it is still only impression.

## The Sweetness That Has to Be Learned

> “Sweeter also than honey and the drippings of the honeycomb.” — Psalm 19:10

> “Your words were found, and I ate them, and your words became to me a joy and the delight of my heart.” — Jeremiah 15:16

I want to tell the truth here: sweetness is not always where I arrive on any given morning.

There are readings that feel like entering a familiar room where someone I love is waiting. And there are readings that feel like the spiritual equivalent of physical therapy — I do it because I know it is what I need, not because I am experiencing any particular pleasure in the moment.

The lesson’s counsel on this is honest: delight is not an emotion you produce before reading. Delight is a fruit of sustained, repeated contact with the Word over time. The person who has been reading Scripture with attention across years develops an accumulated recognition — of familiar voices, recurring themes, the characteristic mercy of the God who keeps showing up throughout the whole arc of human history.

What defeats most people is expecting delight to arrive instantly and abandoning the practice when it does not. What the lesson invites instead is a patient, sustained return. Not because you have manufactured the right feeling, but because God’s Word — Isaiah 55:11 tells us — does not return empty. It accomplishes what He intends.

*You may not feel it at 5:47 on a Tuesday morning.*

*But it is working.*

## Five Things to Build

- **Time** — protected time, not just more time; abiding cannot happen on fragments
- **Place** — a recurring location that reduces distraction and trains the body toward attention
- **Depth** — prayer, careful reading, writing, and honest application before you close the book
- **Sharing** — what you receive privately becomes stronger when you give it away
- **Delight** — not a prerequisite for study, but a long-term fruit of faithful contact

These are not a program to complete. They are a life to build — slowly, imperfectly, in the company of the Spirit who was given precisely to make Scripture alive and effective in the people who come to it.

Jesus rose before dawn and found a solitary place. The disciples tracked him down, confused, and told him everyone was looking for him. There was more to do. There was always more to do.

**He had already taken care of the most important thing.**
