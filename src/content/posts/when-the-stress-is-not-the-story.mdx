One of the clearest lessons from my recent time in Nigeria came from the stress surrounding Auntie Melody and Uncle James’s 40th wedding anniversary.

In the days leading up to the celebration, the pressure was extraordinary. Auntie Melody in particular seemed to be carrying so much weight that I genuinely wondered how she was going to make it through the event. The logistics were fluid. The timeline was shifting. The demands felt relentless.

Yet when the day arrived, everything came together.

Family and friends gathered. People laughed and celebrated. The couple was honored, and the occasion became beautiful and memorable.

The stress was real.

But the stress was not the story.

---

## I. The Noise of the Preparation

As a physician, I am trained to distinguish between clinical noise and the patient's actual trajectory. 

In the intensive care unit or the delivery room, alarms go off constantly. A monitor beeps because a lead is loose; a sensor flashes because of a temporary movement. A junior clinician can easily spend their entire shift chasing the alarms, reacting to every transient fluctuation in heart rate or blood pressure, completely missing the patient’s underlying path to recovery. 

We learn, through experience, to filter out the noise so we can focus on the signal.

The preparation for any major family milestone in Nigeria can feel like a room filled with alarms. From an American perspective—where we try to optimize every variable and eliminate every risk—the chaos of the preparation can look like a looming disaster. It is easy to look at the disorder and conclude that the event is falling apart.

But the preparation does not predict the outcome. 

Physiologically, our bodies are built to handle acute stress responses. But we are not meant to live in them. If we mistake the temporary elevation of cortisol for our permanent state, we miss the homeostasis that follows. The chaos is the noise. 

The gathering is the signal.

---

## II. The Architecture of Margin

The practical lesson here is the necessity of building margin—both temporal and emotional. 

We must arrive early when possible. We must expect plans to change. We must help where we can without letting the surrounding chaos consume our internal peace. Most importantly, we must remember why we came in the first place.

The purpose is not flawless execution.

It is family, presence, celebration, and memory.

This is where Adventist theology offers a profound structural remedy. God did not design human beings to exist in a state of endless preparation and production. He built a boundary directly into the architecture of time. 

The Sabbath is a sanctuary of margin.

Every week, the world demands optimization, labor, and preparation. And every week, the Sabbath commands us to stop—to step out of the chaos of the six days and enter a space where presence is prioritized over performance. It is a weekly reminder that the stress of the work is temporary, but the gathering before God is what remains.

---

## III. The Midwifery of Calling

This lesson connects deeply with a conversation I recently had with Pastor Anonaba.

Our discussion ranged from spiritual experiences and divine guidance to family, ministry, and future projects. He spoke of prayer that carried deep personal significance, including quiet, spiritual impressions concerning his family and the naming of a child. We reflected on moments when divine intervention seemed evident in both his personal life and his professional journey.

We also spoke about his larger vision: a hope to someday develop a school for training midwives in Nigeria. 

As we talked about this dream, I was struck by how much it mirrored the anniversary experience. Before anything meaningful comes together, there is almost always an unfinished, chaotic, and uncertain period. 

A celebration must first be prepared.

A naming must first be prayed over.

A school must first exist as a quiet, unseen idea.

In my work in maternal-fetal medicine, this is a daily clinical reality. Labor is loud. It is painful. It is physically and emotionally exhausting. A mother’s body undergoes an extraordinary, intense struggle. To an outside observer who does not understand the process, it might look like a medical crisis in progress. 

But the labor is not the story.

The child is the story.

The midwifery of calling works the same way. The preparation, the labor, and the early periods of uncertainty are not signs of God’s absence. They are the necessary, sometimes painful steps of bringing something new to birth.

---

## IV. The Anatomy of a Faithful Step

Perhaps the spiritual lesson is not to judge the future too quickly by the disorder of the present.

We are often tempted to look at the incomplete areas of our lives—our struggling families, our unfinished projects, our uncertain callings—and conclude that the plan has failed. We treat the temporary stress as the final verdict. 

But prayer does not remove the need for preparation, and vision does not replace action. 

Sometimes, our responsibility is simply to pray, prepare, and take the next faithful step. We do not need to see the finished story to trust the Author. We need enough faith to continue writing our part of it.

The stress is temporary. The gathering remains.

## Questions for Reflection

* How can we learn to distinguish between temporary "pre-celebration" stress and the true value of the relationships we are preparing for?
* In what ways can we build better emotional and spiritual margin into our weekly routines to resist the hurry of the culture?
* When looking at an unfinished dream or calling, how do we prevent the chaotic "labor" of the present from overshadowing the promise of the future?
* What does it look like to trust God's providence when the logistics of our lives seem to be falling apart?
* What has God placed in our hands today that, with prayer and faithful action, might become a blessing to another generation?
