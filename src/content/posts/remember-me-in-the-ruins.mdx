There are consultations where the damage cannot be undone.

The pregnancy has already suffered what it cannot recover from. The path from earlier to now is, in retrospect, legible — we can trace the decision points. We do not say this in the room. Our clinical obligation is not to catalogue the road not taken. It is to work honestly with what remains and to offer what is still genuinely available.

Those are among the hardest rooms I sit in. And they taught me something about what Judges 16 is actually doing.

---

## The Kind of Failure That Cannot Be Managed

There is a kind of failure that can still be hidden. A person can tidy it, rename it, explain it, promise it will not happen again, and return to public life with the appearance of continuity.

Then there is the kind that cannot be managed by reputation.

The eyes are gone. The chains are real. The prison is public. The people who once feared this man now bring him out as entertainment at their religious festival. There is no plausible way to describe what has happened as a misunderstanding. Samson is living in the consequences of what he chose.

It would be easier if Scripture ended his story at the haircut. The moral would be clean: compromise destroys, so do not compromise. That is true, but it is not all the truth the Bible tells. Instead, the story follows Samson into the place where his strength cannot protect him and his calling cannot be used to flatter him.

It follows him into the ruins.

---

## No Cheap Redemption

The Philistines seize Samson, put out his eyes, bind him with bronze fetters, and force him to grind in prison. The man who had made a public spectacle of Philistine strength becomes the object of Philistine celebration. When the lords assemble to sacrifice to Dagon, they call for Samson so he can perform before them.

We should resist every temptation to hurry through this scene in search of inspiration.

Samson has caused real harm. His choices have placed him where he is. His suffering is not an inconvenience between a bad decision and an uplifting comeback. It is consequence. The Bible does not minimize it, and the gospel does not require us to minimize it.

Grace is not the claim that wreckage was actually victory all along.

For anyone who has failed someone, betrayed a trust, fractured a family, compromised a ministry, or lived long enough with sin that there are consequences no apology can reverse, this matters. Christian hope is not built on pretending damage does not matter. It is built on the astonishing claim that damage does not exhaust God's mercy.

The gospel does not call the wreckage victory. It calls the ruined sinner home.

---

## The Quiet Sentence

Between Samson's capture and the feast in Dagon's temple, Judges places a single observation:

> "However, the hair of his head began to grow again after it had been shaven."
> Judges 16:22, NKJV

It is tempting to read this as if Samson's power were a physical substance slowly replenishing as hair length returned. But that would repeat the misunderstanding his life exposes. His hair was never magic. It was the sign of a consecration that had been abandoned.

So why does the narrator tell us it begins to grow again?

Because in the middle of judgment, the text permits a whisper of possibility. The visible sign that had fallen is returning while Samson can no longer pretend that he saves himself. He is not back in Timnah, confident that desire can be indulged without cost. He is not in Gaza, tearing gates from their hinges. He is blind. He is dependent. He has reached the place where strength is no longer a plausible god.

Ellen White reads this humiliation as a place of repentance. That is a severe mercy: not that God needed to blind Samson in order to be gracious, but that Samson in his ruin finally learned what his strength had allowed him to evade.

There are some truths we only become willing to receive after the illusion of control is gone.

---

## "Remember Me"

At the temple, Samson asks the boy leading him to position him between the pillars. Then he prays:

> "O Lord GOD, remember me, I pray! Strengthen me, I pray, just this once."
> Judges 16:28, NKJV

It is not a polished prayer. His stated desire includes vengeance for his eyes. Even at the end, Samson is not presented as a tidy moral specimen. Yet the prayer contains what had so often been absent from his strongest moments: dependence.

*Remember me.*

The man whose life had been marked by grasping finally asks. The man who had acted as though power were his possession finally speaks to the One from whom all power came. The prayer does not offer a defense. It does not argue that he was mostly faithful or that his gifting should offset his failure.

It is the prayer of someone who no longer has a case to make for himself.

That is why it meets the heart of the gospel.

Romans 3 declares that the law gives knowledge of sin and shuts every mouth, because no one is justified by the deeds of the law. But the same chapter declares that God's righteousness is revealed through faith in Jesus Christ for all who believe. The mouth closed in self-defense may yet open in faith.

Lesson 9, "Sin, the Gospel, and the Law," is right to bring Samson into this theological frame. The law has told the truth about him. He has not been faithful to his consecration. He has not preserved the relationship signified by the sign. He cannot undo that verdict by one final strong act.

But he can call upon God.

And God can hear him.

---

## Judgment With Christ at the Center

Adventists know what it is to speak about judgment. We read Daniel 7 and Revelation 14 with deep seriousness. We believe that human lives matter before the court of heaven, that grace is not indifference to how we live, and that the call to fear God and give Him glory is not disposable religious language.

But Samson is a needed safeguard for the way we preach judgment.

If judgment becomes a message that leaves a repentant sinner staring only at the wreckage of his record, it has not yet been preached in the light of the gospel. If it trains us to hope in our successful performance of religious identity, it has misunderstood both holiness and Christ.

The believer's confidence in judgment is not that the record will show a life that never needed mercy. It is that the record of sin has been met by the righteousness of Jesus Christ, received through faith, and that grace is truly transforming those who belong to Him.

This is why the final comparison with Christ must be stated clearly.

Jesus was not a Nazirite in the Numbers 6 sense. He drank from the fruit of the vine and came eating and drinking. Nor does His being from Nazareth make Him a Nazirite; those are different realities. But Christ is what Samson could never be: the perfectly faithful Son, wholly devoted to the Father's will, obedient not because He fell and returned, but obedient unto death.

Samson dies pulling down a temple in a final act bound up with both judgment and his own damaged motives. Jesus gives His life for His enemies and rises again, bearing a righteousness sinners can never construct for themselves.

Our hope is not that we can become impressive versions of Samson. Our hope is that Christ receives failed people who cry out for mercy.

---

## The Name in Hebrews

Then comes the final astonishment.

Centuries after Judges records all of this, Hebrews 11 names Samson among those who through faith endured and acted in the history of God's people. The writer does not retell the story. He does not deny the scandal. He simply includes the name.

*Samson.*

Not because his failure did not matter. Not because his last moment erased every wound caused by his earlier life. Not because a destructive death should be turned into a sentimental lesson.

His name remains because grace is allowed to tell its truth after sin has told its truth.

That is a gospel word for the person who fears that the worst chapter has become the only name heaven knows for them. The inclusion of Samson does not offer cheap reassurance to those determined to keep compromising. It offers real hope to those who have been brought low enough to stop defending themselves and turn again toward God.

There is a difference between presumption and faith. Presumption says, *I will go out as before.* Faith says, *Remember me.*

---

## When You Cannot Undo It

Some readers will come to Samson's last prayer with a relatively clean public life and a quiet secret fear that their strength has become their substitute for God. The invitation is to surrender now, before the symbol and reality part company.

Others will read from somewhere closer to prison. Something has been exposed. Something has been lost. The consequences are neither imaginary nor reversible by religious enthusiasm. The question is no longer how to prevent the past. It is whether there is any faithful prayer left to pray now.

There is.

Do not make your consequence your savior by believing you must suffer enough to pay what only Christ can cover. Do not make your shame your identity by insisting that your sin can speak more finally than God's mercy. Do not call the wreckage small. Bring it honestly into the presence of the One whose righteousness is sufficient for ruined people.

Say the prayer without ornament.

*Remember me.*

The last word over Samson is not the razor. It is not the chains. It is not even the fallen temple. It is a name, retained in the testimony of faith after every reason for self-confidence had collapsed.

The God who remembered him is not finished receiving broken people yet.
