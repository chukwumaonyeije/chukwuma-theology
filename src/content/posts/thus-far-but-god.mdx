*Remembering God's help while we wait for what comes next.*

You can be grateful for what God has done and still be afraid of what comes next.

You remember a season when help came. Someone stayed with you. A prayer was answered. You found strength to do what the day required. You can speak honestly about the ways God has carried you.

But there is another concern now. Your child is still struggling. A relationship still needs repair. You are praying about something you cannot fix. You give your testimony on Sabbath, and that night you lie awake with the same question you brought to church.

Does that uncertainty make your gratitude less sincere?

I think many of us quietly assume it does. We feel that remembering God's faithfulness should settle every fear. When it does not, we begin to question ourselves. After everything He has done, why am I still worried?

Samuel's words leave room for a person in that position: “Thus far the Lord has helped us” (1 Samuel 7:12). He could name the help already given without knowing everything that would happen next.

In [*Thus Far: The Theology of Ebenezer*](/chukwuma-theology/posts/thus-far-the-theology-of-ebenezer/), I reflected on those words after sharing a vespers devotional at the Atlanta North Seventh-day Adventist Church Family Retreat at Ridgecrest Conference Center in Black Mountain, North Carolina. We considered why families need to remember where God has helped them.

Here I want to stay with the person who remembers—and still needs help.

## The stone does not answer every question

Samuel raised Ebenezer after a frightening encounter with the Philistines. Israel had gathered at Mizpah to return to the Lord, confess their sin, and seek His help. Then an enemy approached while they were gathered there.

Their return to God had not removed the danger. They asked Samuel to keep praying for them. God answered, and afterward Samuel set up a stone to mark the help they had received.

The name Ebenezer means “stone of help.” It gave the people a place to remember what had happened and whom they had depended on.

There would be other battles. Samuel had no reason to think that Israel's difficulties were over. He raised the stone because God had helped them in this one.

That is a more modest claim than we sometimes want a testimony to make. We want the answered prayer to guarantee the next answer. We want the story of recovery to explain why someone else did not recover. We want yesterday's deliverance to tell us exactly what God will do tomorrow.

The stone cannot carry all of that.

It can help us remember that our present fear is not everything we have known of God. There have been mercies. There have been people through whom help came. There have been days we did not know how we would face, and we were given enough strength to face them.

We can give thanks for those things while admitting that we still have questions.

Gratitude does not require us to pretend the waiting is easy.

## Why Paul says “But God”

The words “But God” bring us to another passage, and we need to let that passage tell us what they mean.

Ephesians 2 begins with a difficult description of human life apart from Christ. Paul tells his readers that they were dead in trespasses and sins. They were following the course of the world, living under desires that could not give them life, and sharing in a condition that included Paul himself. He says “we all” were there (verses 1–3).

He is describing something deeper than a disappointing season. Sin has damaged our relationship with God. We cannot educate, discipline, or accomplish our way into the life we need.

Then Paul writes:

> “But God, who is rich in mercy, because of His great love with which He loved us, even when we were dead in trespasses, made us alive together with Christ (by grace you have been saved).”
>
> — Ephesians 2:4–5, NKJV

Read the timing carefully. God loved us while we were still dead in our sins. His mercy did not begin after we had made ourselves useful or respectable. He acted when we could not give ourselves life.

And what He gives is life with Christ. Paul goes on to speak of being raised with Him and seated with Him in the heavenly places (verse 6). Our hope rests in our belonging to the crucified and risen Jesus. What He has done for us is greater than what we could accomplish for ourselves.

This is why Paul can say that salvation is by grace through faith, the gift of God, with no room for boasting (verses 8–9). We receive what God gives. We do not become its authors.

Then comes verse 10: we are God's workmanship, created in Christ Jesus for good works. Grace gives rise to a changed life. The order matters. We are loved and made alive in Christ, and from that life we learn to walk in the good God calls us to do.

For people accustomed to working hard, that can take time to accept. We know how to prepare, meet expectations, and prove that we can be trusted with responsibility. Those habits serve us well in many parts of life. But we can carry them into prayer until even our relationship with God feels like another place where we must earn our standing.

Paul gives us a different starting point. God is rich in mercy. He loves us. We come to Him because we need the life He gives.

You do not have to make yourself impressive before you can ask for help.

## When the prayer is still unanswered

This matters because “But God” can become a phrase we use too quickly around another person's pain.

Someone tells us what is happening in their family, and we rush toward the turnaround. We want to reassure them that the diagnosis will change, the marriage will recover, or the child will come home. We may mean to encourage them. But we cannot promise an outcome God has not promised.

Ephesians 2 does not say that every difficult circumstance will soon be reversed. It says that God has acted in Christ to save people who could not save themselves.

That gives us something substantial to hold while we wait. Our suffering does not make us ineligible for His love. Our need has not surprised Him. We can bring the whole truth of our condition to the God whose mercy met us before we had anything to offer.

For the person who is waiting, that may change the way prayer sounds.

“Lord, I believe You love me. I do not understand what is happening.”

“Thank You for the help You have given. I am tired, and I need help again.”

“I know what I am asking You to do. I do not know what I will do if the answer is different.”

These are prayers we should be able to say in church, at family worship, and alone in the dark. We do not have to remove the difficult sentence before speaking to God.

The same honesty should shape how we care for each other. A person who has prayed for years may need us to listen longer than we expected. Someone grieving may need practical help after everyone else has returned to normal. A family in crisis may need prayer, appropriate counsel, and people willing to remain involved.

We can become part of the help we are asking God to send.

## The future we have been promised

There is a question we cannot avoid here. What about the person whose circumstances never improve? What about the prayer followed by a funeral?

If Christian hope depends entirely on receiving the outcome we want in this life, there are graves beside which we will have nothing truthful to say.

The resurrection of Jesus gives us more to say, though it does not remove the need to weep. Christ entered our suffering and death, and He rose. The life Paul describes in Ephesians belongs to a Savior who has overcome the grave.

As Seventh-day Adventists, we look forward to His return and the resurrection of those who have died in Christ. Paul describes that hope in 1 Thessalonians 4:13–18 so that grieving believers can encourage one another. Their grief is real. So is the promised reunion.

That promise matters when we cannot point to a happy ending here. We do not know when every present burden will lift. We do know that death will not keep forever those whom Christ calls from the grave. God's purpose for His people reaches beyond the years we can presently see.

Until then, we keep asking for healing. We seek reconciliation where it is possible. We care for the sick, support the grieving, and do the good that is before us. Hope in Christ's return gives us reason to stay faithful in that work, even when its results are incomplete.

We can trust Him with a future we will not be able to finish ourselves.

## Bring both prayers

The gospel song [“But God”](https://music.apple.com/us/song/but-god/334974503), recorded by Ron Winans and featuring BeBe Winans, offers a musical companion to this reflection. As you listen, keep Paul's words nearby: God is rich in mercy, and He loved us when we could not give ourselves life.

Then consider making room for two sentences at your next family worship:

> “I remember when God helped us by…”

> “The place where I still need God's help is…”

Let each person answer without requiring the second sentence to sound as confident as the first. A child may name something an adult did not realize was troubling them. Someone may remember a kindness the rest of the family has forgotten. Someone may only be able to say, “I am still waiting.”

Pray both. Leave room for the person who can only manage the second.

That person belongs in the circle too. They do not need to borrow someone else's testimony or predict how their own story will turn out. They can bring their need to the same God whose help the family remembers.

Thus far the Lord has helped us.

We can ask Him for help again today.
