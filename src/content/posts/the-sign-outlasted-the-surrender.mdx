I have sat with patients who describe their symptoms as "normal for me."

The accommodation happened in stages. What was once alarming became tolerable, then expected, then simply background. By the time they sit across from me, the compromised state is no longer experienced as a compromise. They have built an entire life around a problem they have ceased to recognize as one.

Samson's story in Judges 14 through 16 is that process, written in slow motion.

---

## Not One Terrible Moment

We tend to remember Samson through a single scene: Delilah, a sleeping man, a razor, a sudden loss of strength. It makes for dramatic storytelling. It also risks misreading the spiritual anatomy of his fall.

Judges does not present a good man ruined by one unsuspected betrayal. It presents a man whose desires repeatedly draw him into the world he was called to confront.

In Judges 14, Samson sees a Philistine woman in Timnah and tells his parents to get her for him because she pleases him. His parents ask whether there is no woman among his own people. The concern is not ethnic disdain but covenant identity: Samson is the designated beginning of deliverance from Philistine oppression, yet he is determined to bind himself to Philistine desire.

Later in the same chapter, he turns aside to the carcass of the lion he had killed and takes honey from the dead body. For an ordinary Israelite, the act would involve impurity. For a Nazirite, whose calling explicitly required separation from the dead, it exposes how easily he now moves past the sacred boundaries of his life.

Then comes Gaza. Then Delilah. Then the strange and terrible game in which Samson repeatedly stays with a woman who openly tests the source of his strength for the benefit of his enemies.

The haircut is not the beginning of his problem. It is the public revelation of what his life has been practicing in private.

The tragedy is not that Samson's hair was finally cut. It is that his heart had been negotiating long before the razor arrived.

---

## When Calling Becomes Presumption

Samson is difficult to read because the Spirit of the Lord does work through him. He accomplishes acts of deliverance. He experiences astonishing strength. In Judges 15, he kills one thousand Philistines with the jawbone of a donkey. The text is not embarrassed by his power.

But power is not the same thing as approval.

This distinction is one of the most important spiritual disciplines a church can learn. We too readily infer character from effectiveness. The sermon moved people, so the preacher must be safe. The ministry grew, so its leader must be spiritually healthy. The physician is brilliant, the administrator decisive, the family impressive, the influence undeniable. We assume the interior life must be sound.

Samson warns us that gifting can continue long enough for a person to mistake divine patience for divine endorsement. God bears patiently. God advances His purposes despite compromised instruments. But that patience is not a license to turn calling into presumption.

The Sabbath School lesson on "Sin, the Gospel, and the Law" reaches directly for this nerve. Samson fell, it observes, because he thought he was strong. He depended on his own strength to overcome temptation.

Not the absence of strength. The misuse of it.

The ability God gave him became the very ground from which he imagined he could afford not to depend on God.

---

## The Law as Merciful Diagnosis

Contemporary Christianity is often tempted to speak about sin in one of two distorted ways. We can name it harshly, as if the naming were itself the gospel. Or we can soften it into vague brokenness, as if clarity were unkind.

There is a better path. God's law is not a rival to God's love. It is a disclosure of what love requires, and therefore an honest account of what destroys relationship with God and neighbor.

> "Through the law comes the knowledge of sin."
> Romans 3:20, ESV

The law is the diagnostic light. It names the disease we would rather continue calling normal.

I do not serve a patient by concealing a dangerous finding because the truth will be emotionally difficult. Diagnosis can be painful. It can also be the beginning of rescue. The law's clarity is mercy when it sends us away from self-trust and toward Christ.

Samson needed that clarity. So do we.

His problem is not simply that he crossed technical lines. It is that he was learning, choice by choice, to regard God's claim upon him as negotiable. He could touch the carcass and still be strong. He could seek intimacy in destructive places and still escape. He could reveal himself slowly to danger and still assume that strength would arrive when summoned.

After his hair is cut, Judges records this:

> "And he awoke from his sleep, and said, 'I will go out as at other times, and shake myself free.' But he did not know that the LORD had departed from him."
> Judges 16:20, NKJV

There may be no more frightening sentence in the whole narrative. Not his captivity. Not his blindness. The fact that he expected nothing to be different.

A life of incremental compromise produces exactly this: a person who has fully normalized catastrophe.

---

## The Hair Was Never Magic

Ellen White's treatment of Samson in *Patriarchs and Prophets* refuses superstition. There was no intrinsic virtue in his long hair, she explains. It was a token of loyalty to God. The symbol mattered because the relationship mattered.

That is exactly the balance a Seventh-day Adventist reader should hold.

Visible signs matter. The Sabbath matters. Baptism matters. Temperance matters. Faithful habits matter. Truth lived in the body and the calendar is not an embarrassment to biblical faith. A spirituality with no outward form is too thin to be biblical.

But a sign cannot save us from the God we are no longer willing to trust.

It is possible to keep Sabbath hours while allowing resentment, appetite, ambition, or self-importance to erode the Sabbath's central confession: I am not my own; my time belongs to God. It is possible to defend health principles while nourishing an uncontrolled inner life. It is possible to bear the name of a remnant people while growing more confident in being right than dependent on Jesus.

The sign is not empty because it is visible. The sign becomes empty when it is separated from surrender.

Samson's hair did not betray him. It told the truth about what he had already given away.

---

## The Enemy Rarely Needs an Explosion

We often imagine temptation as a sudden demand to renounce God at once. For most people, most of the time, that is not how spiritual ruin arrives.

It arrives as fatigue with limits. Familiarity with risk. Confidence that we can remain near what is undoing us without being finally undone.

Delilah does not conquer Samson in a single conversation. Judges 16 gives the exhausting repetition: questions, evasions, tests, pressure, and his steady refusal to leave. He treats a mortal threat as a manageable relationship.

This is why Jesus speaks so severely in the Sermon on the Mount about removing what causes sin. His language is not a command for physical self-harm. It is an insistence that nothing which destroys communion with God should be granted the status of a harmless companion.

There are relationships we should stop romanticizing. Screens we should stop treating as neutral. Patterns of spending, eating, talking, desiring, or working that have become forms of spiritual numbness. The question is not whether we can stand near them and remain impressive for a while.

Samson could.

The question is whether they are training the heart to surrender.

---

## When the Mirror Turns Toward Us

It is tempting to make Samson an exotic failure: a violent ancient figure with a weakness for dangerous company. It is more faithful to let the text move closer.

Where do I still appear consecrated while resisting surrender?

Where have I mistaken effectiveness for faithfulness?

What have I repeated because the consequences were delayed long enough to feel like permission?

What part of my spiritual identity has become a symbol I display rather than a claim I receive with humility?

We do not ask these questions because we enjoy shame. We ask them because the law of God is not exposure for its own sake. It is the searching grace that refuses to let us sleep safely while our loyalties are being quietly replaced.

The gospel will come. It must come. Samson's story does not end in Delilah's room.

But before grace restores, truth speaks.

The locks on the floor did not begin Samson's fall. They revealed it. And if the sign still remains on us today, the mercy is that there is still time to return to the One it was always meant to signify.
