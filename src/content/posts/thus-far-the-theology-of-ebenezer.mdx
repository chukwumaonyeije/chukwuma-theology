*Faith does not know everything that comes next. It remembers who brought us here.*

*I first shared a shorter version of this reflection as a vespers devotional during the Atlanta North Seventh-day Adventist Church Family Retreat at Ridgecrest Conference Center in Black Mountain, North Carolina. Among families, children, friends, and several generations of our church community, two words seemed especially appropriate: Thus far.*

Fear is often better at keeping records than faith.

We remember the diagnosis, the financial crisis, the night the phone rang. We can recall what someone said twenty years ago at a family gathering. When another uncertainty arrives, those memories are ready. They supply the evidence for everything we suspect might go wrong.

The quieter memories sometimes need help: the person who stayed, the strength to get through one more day, the forgiveness we once thought impossible. These belong to our story too. Yet we can move past them so quickly that, when fear begins its argument, grace seems to have no witnesses.

In 1 Samuel 7, Samuel gives Israel something that will help them remember.

> “Then Samuel took a stone and set it up between Mizpah and Shen, and called its name Ebenezer, saying, ‘Thus far the Lord has helped us.’”
>
> — 1 Samuel 7:12

Ebenezer means “stone of help.” Samuel names a place where Israel can look back and acknowledge what God has done. There will be more history beyond this stone. More uncertainty. More occasions to pray without knowing how the answer will come.

But here, at this point in the story, they can speak truthfully: **Thus far the Lord has helped us.**

## A stone after the crisis

The background matters. Earlier in 1 Samuel, Israel had carried the ark of the covenant into battle as though its presence could secure a victory. The ark was captured. Israel learned, painfully, that possessing a sacred object did not give them control over God.

By chapter 7, Samuel is calling the people to return to the Lord with all their hearts. They must put away their rival gods, the Baals and Ashtoreths, and direct their worship to Him. At Mizpah, they gather, fast, and confess their sin. Samuel intercedes for them.

Then the Philistines come.

There is something painfully recognizable about that sequence. You make the difficult decision to return to God. You begin to repair what has been neglected. And another threat appears.

Faithfulness does not eliminate conflict.

This time, the people ask Samuel to keep crying out to the Lord on their behalf. God answers. The Lord thunders against the Philistines, throwing them into confusion, and Israel prevails.

Samuel raises his stone after the deliverance. Its name directs the people's attention to the source of their help. In years to come, when the details of the battle begin to fade, Ebenezer will still bear witness: we needed God here, and He helped us.

Remembrance gives gratitude somewhere to stand.

## Stones that invite a question

In Joshua 4, twelve stones taken from the Jordan become a memorial to Israel's crossing. Their purpose extends beyond the people who walked through the river. In time, children will ask what the stones mean, and another generation will hear how God brought His people through.

The question belongs to the design. A child notices something. An adult pauses to explain. A remembered act of God becomes a story someone else can carry.

That is a serious responsibility for a family. Our children hear our instructions. They learn our expectations. They may know which day we worship, what we believe about the Second Coming, and which choices we hope they will make. They also need to hear where we have learned to trust God.

They need the parts we sometimes edit out: we were frightened; we did not know what to do; we asked for help. They need to know how faith was practiced when the outcome was still uncertain.

A testimony becomes credible when it tells the truth about the need as well as the help.

We should leave room for their questions, too. The stones in Joshua invite conversation. Our stories should make it possible for a child to ask about God, including the things we cannot yet explain.

## The altar that needed repair

Another gathering of stones appears in 1 Kings 18. We remember Mount Carmel for the fire from heaven. Before that public answer, however, Elijah calls the people near and repairs the broken altar of the Lord.

He uses twelve stones, corresponding to the tribes descended from Jacob. The kingdom is divided, but the altar recalls the people God called into covenant with Himself. Even in their compromised worship, that history still addresses them.

**God's claim upon His people is older than their wandering.**

The repaired altar gives visible form to Elijah's call to return. The people are being confronted with a relationship they have neglected and a God who is calling them back.

There is a family application here, though we should be careful with it. Repairing an altar is no formula for obtaining a miracle. God does not owe us a dramatic answer once we have organized our devotional life.

Still, neglected worship deserves our attention. Prayer can become occasional. Sabbath can fill with everything except rest in God. An unresolved injury can make the people under one roof feel very far apart.

Repair may begin quietly: an apology without a defense, a brief prayer together, a passage of Scripture read without turning it into a lecture. These are ways of responding to the God who has already called us near.

## Remembering without erasing grief

At a family retreat, it is natural to speak of the ways God has carried us. Yet every gathering also holds stories that have not resolved as people hoped.

A loved one died. A relationship ended. A child is still struggling. Someone came to worship with a prayer they have been praying for years.

We must not make Ebenezer a demand that every family produce a happy ending. Nor can survival become a measure of whose faith was sufficient. Christian testimony has to be honest enough to include an empty chair.

Sometimes the help we remember is the friend who sat with us through grief. Sometimes it is the ability to pray through anger, or the discovery that we could bring our questions to God without first making them respectable. We may be able to name that help while still lamenting what happened.

“Thus far” leaves room for an unfinished story.

Our deepest confidence rests in the God revealed in Jesus Christ, who entered human suffering, bore death, and rose again. His resurrection gives Christian hope a future beyond the outcomes we can presently see. As Adventists, our expectation of Christ's return keeps that future before us: death and separation will not have the last word.

We remember particular mercies within that larger promise. We do not have to explain every loss before we can receive the help that comes today.

## A memorial in time

For Seventh-day Adventists, remembrance already has a weekly place in our lives.

The Sabbath commandment in Exodus 20 points back to Creation. In Deuteronomy 5, Sabbath observance is also connected to Israel's deliverance from slavery. The people are to remember both their Creator and the God who brought them out of bondage.

Every seventh day, we receive time in which to remember whose we are.

In that sense, Sabbath can serve as a weekly Ebenezer. We arrive at Friday evening with unfinished work, unresolved questions, and responsibilities that will still be waiting. We stop because our lives are held by God even when our tasks are incomplete.

That practice challenges a habit many of us have cultivated: treating our usefulness as the measure of our worth. Sabbath teaches us to receive life before we try to manage it. Rest becomes an embodied confession of dependence.

Thus far the Lord has helped us. We can entrust these hours to Him too.

## Who gets remembered in our story?

Adventists often describe the conflict between Christ and Satan as the Great Controversy. At its center are questions about God's character and whether He can be trusted.

Those questions enter ordinary life. Suffering can make trust difficult. So can success, though we may notice it less readily.

After a crisis passes, we sometimes revise the account. We remember our judgment, our persistence, our ability to adapt. Gradually, the prayers and the help we received disappear from the telling. God becomes a footnote in an autobiography about ourselves.

Ebenezer interrupts that revision. It teaches us to give thanks for the skill, relationships, opportunities, and strength through which help came. Acknowledging God's providence can deepen our gratitude for the people who served us and the work they did.

The stone asks us to tell the fuller story.

## Tell the children where the stones are

During the retreat, I asked families to turn toward one another and answer a simple question: **Where has God helped our family?**

The question deserves a specific answer. Name a season. Recall a person. Tell what frightened you and how help arrived. If part of the story remains painful, allow that truth into the conversation.

At your next family worship, try completing this sentence together:

> “I remember when God helped our family by…”

Write the answers down. Let children contribute their own memories. Leave space for someone whose honest response is, “I am still waiting.” Pray for that need together.

You might also ask what needs repair in your life together, and choose one small act of faithfulness you can begin this week.

These conversations give the next generation something to carry. They may also restore a memory you need yourself. Hearing the story aloud can remind you that the fear in front of you is not the whole account of your life.

Another uncertain season will come. The stone Samuel raised offers no timetable for what comes next. It bears witness to the help already given.

The God who has brought us this far has not exhausted Himself getting us here.

Tell the story. Keep a place for it. One day, when someone in your family needs courage for the next step, they may reach for the memory you preserved.

**Thus far the Lord has helped us.**
