## PART THREE: POST-RUN-THROUGH SELF-EVALUATION

*Sit with these questions honestly. Not harshly. Elijah sat under his juniper tree and God did not scold him for being tired. These questions are for clarity, not condemnation.*

---

### On the Medical Illustrations

**Did the HELLP Syndrome illustration feel natural — or like a CV?**

The line you are looking for: *Do I sound like a physician who became a preacher, or a preacher who happens to know medicine?* The illustration exists to give the congregation a concrete picture of collapse-after-triumph. If it felt like you were explaining your credentials, shorten the clinical detail. If it landed as an "aha" — leave it exactly where it is.

**Did the obstetric triage intake question illuminate, or did it pull the congregation away from Elijah?**

The test: when you asked *"How are you — really?"* in Point 2, did it feel like it came from the text, or like a detour to the hospital? If there was any sense of departure, tighten the bridge back to the still small voice.

**Was the ECG 'quiet finding' analogy clear enough to land without being explained twice?**

A good analogy explains itself the first time. If you found yourself repeating the comparison, it needs one cleaner sentence at setup.

---

### On the Vulnerable Moments

**Was the stillbirth story delivered slowly enough?**

That story carries weight no pace can outrun. If the run-through moved through it quickly — if you felt yourself accelerating to get through the discomfort — plan to physically slow your breathing before you enter that passage on Sabbath. That moment deserves silence before and after. Let the congregation stay with it.

**Did the 'I thought I was the only one' personal reflection feel authentic or performed?**

There is no formula here. You know the difference when you hear it in your own voice. If it felt performed, ask yourself: *Am I describing what I actually felt, or what I think vulnerability is supposed to sound like?* Strip it back to the simplest, truest sentence.

---

### On the Intro and Closing

**Did the intro breathe, or was it rushed?**

The Bronx-to-Braselton arc is doing more than telling a story — it is inviting the congregation to trust you before you take them somewhere difficult. Rushed intros tell the audience: *I'm nervous, let's get to the real material.* A breathing intro says: *We're going somewhere together. I know the way.* If the run-through started at speed, plan a deliberate three-beat pause before your first full sentence.

**Did the closing verse have room to settle?**

1 Kings 19:7 is not a conclusion you explain. It is a conclusion you read — and then step back from. After you say *"because the journey is too great for thee,"* the sermon should already be done. If anything came after it, ask honestly: was that necessary, or was it nerves keeping the sermon from ending?

---

### On the Pastoral Register

**Did the direct address land as pastoral or as performative?**

When you looked up from the text and spoke *to* someone — the person running on empty, the person who just had their Mount Carmel and is now under the juniper — did it feel like you were seeing them, or presenting to them? Pastoral is eye contact and lowered voice. Performative is volume and gesture. Know which you were doing.

---

### On the SDA Content

**Was the Sabbath architecture woven into Point 1, or bolted onto it?**

If you had to stop the sermon's momentum to make the Sabbath connection, it needs to be integrated more smoothly. The congregation should feel it as textual, not topical. The question to ask: *Does this connection emerge from what Elijah needed, or does it interrupt what Elijah experienced?*

**Did the Great Controversy framing in Point 2 serve the text — or compete with it?**

The still small voice is already profound. The GC frame should amplify it, not replace it. If the doctrinal layer felt heavier than the pastoral moment, consider shortening the theological summary to one strong sentence and letting the narrative do the rest.

**Was the remnant theology in Point 3 humble, not boastful?**

This is the most delicate thread in the whole sermon. *"I have left me seven thousand"* is God's comfort to a broken man — not a recruiting poster. If the remnant language in your run-through felt triumphant rather than tender, go back to the tone of the original question: *"What are you doing here, Elijah?"* The answer is not *"You should be thrilled to be part of a remnant."* The answer is: *"You are not alone. You never were."*

**Did the High Priest / Sanctuary intercession land as comfort, not doctrine?**

The test: did the congregation (or would they) exhale at that moment, or lean forward to track a theological argument? If it's landing as comfort, the exhale is there. If it's landing as doctrine, simplify the framing and ground it in the plain emotional truth: *Someone is interceding for you right now. You are not facing this alone.*

---

### On the Hebrew Terms

**rav, qum/nus, qol demamah daqah** — did they land as illumination or as display?

Read the Hebrew. Then immediately translate it into the plainest pastoral sentence you have. The congregation should feel like the Hebrew word gave them permission to feel something — not that they were asked to admire your preparation.

---

## PART FOUR: THE ONE THING

*Before you close your notes, answer this question out loud:*

> **"If every person in the room on Sabbath forgets everything else I said —**
> **what is the one sentence I want them to carry home?"**

Write that sentence here. Say it again. Make sure you could say it in your sleep — because somewhere in that congregation, someone will need to hear it and remember it the rest of their life.

The sermon's answer, as built across seven days of preparation:

---

> **God's first response to your emptiness is not a new assignment. It is a meal.**
> *He comes to where you are. He feeds you before He sends you. And He comes back — a second time — because He knows the journey ahead of you is greater than you think.*

---

That is the sermon. Everything else is scaffolding.

*The people in that room have burdens I don't know.*

*Let me not get in the way.*
*Use what is useful. Set aside what is mine and not Yours.*
*Find the person in that room who most needs to hear that You come back a second time.*
*And let them hear it.*
*I surrender the sermon. I trust the Preacher.*
*Amen.*

## FINAL WORD TO THE PREACHER

You have spent seven days with Elijah under the juniper tree, at the mouth of the cave, in the still small voice. You have done what a faithful shepherd does before he feeds others — you have been fed yourself.

The congregation at Braselton will not grade the exegesis. They will not remember which Hebrew word you used. What they will remember — what they will carry with them into the next hard week of their lives — is whether they felt seen, whether they believed that God is actually like this, and whether someone who had clearly been to the empty place themselves stood before them and said: *He came back. A second time. And the journey was real, and the bread was already baked, and you are not alone.*

That is what you have to give them.

Go preach.
