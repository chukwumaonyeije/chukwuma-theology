# Day 6 of 7 — Intro & Conclusion Delivery Practice
## "The Boy From the Bronx Who Loved a Runner" / "The Bread Is Already Baked"
**Target: Intro 4–6 min + Conclusion 5–7 min | Today: Rehearse each section separately, then run them back-to-back as bookends. These two sections are the sermon. Everything in between is argument. These are the doors.**

---

## PART ONE: THE INTRODUCTION
### "The Boy From the Bronx Who Loved a Runner"

---

## SPEAKER'S FLOW — Key Moments and Pivot Phrases

This is not a script. It is a sequence of arrivals. Each entry below names what you are doing emotionally and structurally — not what words to say. Trust yourself with the words. Know where you are going and why.

---

### BEAT 1 — The Bronx (Entry, ~45 seconds)

**What you are doing:** Placing yourself in a specific geography. Making yourself a person before you become a preacher.

- Start without preamble. No "good morning." No "let us pray again." No warm-up joke.
- Begin mid-thought, as if the congregation is already inside the memory with you.
- The Bronx is not decoration. It is the opening act of a credibility claim: *this preacher knows what it is to grow up in a world that is loud and hard and concrete, and still find God in it.*
- Name the neighborhood. Name the borough. Let it be specific enough to be real.
- **Pivot phrase:** *"We moved to Liverpool, New York."*

---

### BEAT 2 — Liverpool, NY (The Pause that lands, ~20 seconds)

**What you are doing:** Creating the first congregational moment. Let the smile happen.

- Say it plainly: *Liverpool, New York.*
- Then — stop. Do not explain it. Do not say "not England." Do not add anything for a full breath.
- Let them figure it out. Let the small ripple of recognition move through the room.
- THEN — gently, almost conspiratorially — *"Not England."*
- This pause is not a comedy beat. It is a trust move. You are inviting the congregation to be playful with you. They will follow you into harder emotional territory later because you let them laugh here first.
- **Do not milk it.** Two or three seconds of their smile is enough. Move.
- **Pivot phrase:** *"The church was called Mount Carmel."*

---

### BEAT 3 — Mount Carmel SDA Church (The Name Lands, ~30 seconds)

**What you are doing:** Planting the first thematic stake without announcing it.

- Say the name and let a beat of quiet follow.
- The congregation that knows the text will feel the electricity of that name. The congregation that doesn't will feel it later — and will be glad they were there when you said it first.
- You do not need to say: *"Notice the name."* The pause says it.
- Speak warmly about the church itself. This is not a setup. This is a real memory — the smell of the building, the sound of the congregation, the feel of a Sabbath that shaped you.
- **Pivot phrase:** *"And in that church, I heard about Elijah."*

---

### BEAT 4 — The Child Who Loved Chapter 18 (~60–75 seconds)

**What you are doing:** Establishing your emotional entry point into the text — and letting it be a child's entry point, not a scholar's. This disarms the congregation and earns you the right to exegete later.

- Elijah was everything to the boy in the Bronx. Not because he prayed — because he was *fast.* Because he *won.* Because he stood up alone against impossible odds.
- Name what Carmel felt like at age eight or ten: the fire coming down, the crowd falling on its face, the race to Jezreel — *this man outran a chariot.*
- Let the child's logic be completely valid. It *was* great. Carmel *was* magnificent. The boy was not wrong to love it.
- **Key moment:** Speak briefly about what kind of faith a child builds from a story like that — a faith that believes God shows up in the spectacular. Powerful. Fast. Winning.
- Do not critique the child's theology yet. That is not this beat.
- **Pivot phrase:** *"It wasn't until college that I sat down with chapter 19."*

---

### BEAT 5 — The College Discovery (The Real Turn, ~45 seconds)

**What you are doing:** Narrating the moment the sermon was born in you. This is the intellectual and spiritual hinge.

- Something changed when you sat with chapter 19. Not because you had a crisis — because you read it slowly for the first time.
- What you found there was not what you expected. This was not a victory chapter. This was a rest chapter. A bread chapter. A silence chapter.
- Let the contrast carry the weight: *The chapter after the greatest day in Elijah's career — and God does not give him more fire. God gives him sleep.*
- **The statement that opens the sermon proper:**
  > *"Chapter 19 is not about fire. It is about bread. It is about sleep. It is about a still small voice. It is about a God who does not abandon broken people."*
- Say this last sentence slowly. Each item in the list should have a breath of its own.
- After the final phrase — *a God who does not abandon broken people* — a full beat of silence.
- **Do not move immediately into exegesis.** This sentence is where you shift from personal to proclamatory. Let the room feel the shift.

---

### TONE NOTE FOR THE FULL INTRODUCTION

The intro should feel like settling into a chair. There is no urgency yet. There is no thesis to defend. You are welcoming the congregation into a memory, and then — at the very end — pivoting to reveal what that memory is actually about.

**What to resist:**
- Preaching before you've told the story.
- Moving to the text before you've anchored the personal.
- Any sentence that begins with "Today I want to tell you..." — you are already telling them. Let the story be the sermon's beginning, not an announcement of it.
- Speed. The single most common error in introductions is treating them as the on-ramp to the real sermon. **This intro IS the real sermon's first act.** The congregation's openness for the next forty minutes depends on whether they feel they are in safe hands in the first five.

---

## CHAPTER 18 BRIDGE — TRANSITION INTO THE BODY (1–2 min)

This is the seam between intro and exegesis. It must be felt, not merely recited.

### The Setup
You've just placed the congregation in the memory — the child at Mount Carmel SDA, the discovery of chapter 19. Now you give them the chapter 18 summary not as background information but as *contrast fuel.*

Say it with admiration, not as backdrop:
> *"One prophet. Four hundred and fifty prophets of Baal. A drought, a mountain, a fire from heaven. The nation on its knees. And then Elijah ran — ran — in the Spirit of God to Jezreel ahead of the chariot of Ahab. By any measure: his greatest day."*

### The Pivot
Take one breath. Lower your register slightly.

> *"And then Jezebel sent a message."*

Pause.

> *"One sentence. From one woman."*

Pause again. Let the asymmetry be uncomfortable.

**Then, direct address — looking up from the text:**
> *"The distance between your greatest victory and your deepest collapse can be one bad message. One phone call. One email. One conversation after church."*

Say this last line with weight, not speed. The congregation needs to feel the specificity of it. They will hear whichever version is theirs. Let silence hold the room for a moment before moving into the first point.

## PART TWO: THE CONCLUSION
### "The Bread Is Already Baked"

---

## THE CLOSING APPEAL — BEAT BY BEAT WITH PACING NOTES

The conclusion has three distinct movements. Each has its own register, pacing, and task. Do not collapse them together. Let each movement breathe before the next begins.

---

### MOVEMENT 1 — Return to the Boy (Bookend the Intro, ~60–75 seconds)

**Register:** Warm. Quiet. Like finishing a letter.

**What you are doing:** Closing the frame. The sermon that opened in a Bronx-adjacent childhood is now returning there — but the boy is older, and he understands something new.

- Return without announcing the return. Do not say "remember what I told you at the beginning." Simply step back into the image. The congregation will recognize it and feel the satisfaction of the architecture.
- What did that boy not yet understand when he loved Elijah's chariot race?
  - He did not understand that the greatest thing Elijah ever did was not chapter 18.
  - He did not understand that the bravest sentence in the entire Elijah narrative is not *"Answer me, O LORD, answer me"* — it is *"It is enough."*
  - The courage to admit you are done is not smaller than the courage to call fire from heaven. In some ways, it is harder.

**Pivot phrase to close this movement:**
> *"What makes Elijah great is not his capacity. It is his honesty."*

Say it and hold it. This sentence is one of the sermon's load-bearing beams. Let it settle before you build on it.

---

### MOVEMENT 2 — The Reframe of Greatness (~90 seconds)

**Register:** Declaratory, but not loud. This is not shouting. It is revealing.

**Pacing note: Each contrast below is a separate beat. Say the first clause, pause, say the second. Do not rush the list.**

> *"Not the fire — the bread.*
>
> *Not the chariot race — the sleep.*
>
> *Not the four hundred and fifty prophets — the one angel.*
>
> *Not the mountain spectacle — the thin silence."*

After the list: lower your voice rather than raising it.

> *"He did not perform wellness he did not feel. He lay down. He said: it is enough. And God met him — there. Not after he got up. Not once he had pulled himself together. Not once he had something to show for it.*
>
> *There. In the dust. Under a tree. Done."*

**Where to slow most:** On the word *there.* The first time you say it, it is a location. The second time, it is a theology. The third time — *"There. In the dust."* — it is a pastoral declaration. Let each "there" be slightly slower and lower than the last.

**Do not rush out of this moment.** You are making the central claim of the entire sermon: that divine presence precedes divine power, that grace comes to the floor, that God does not need us to be functional before He feeds us. Let the congregation receive it.

---

### MOVEMENT 3 — The Direct Address (The Pastoral Moment, ~90–120 seconds)

**Register:** Intimate. Almost private. As if you are speaking to one person.

**Pacing note:** Slow down more here than anywhere else in the sermon except the stillbirth moment in Point 3. You are not arguing. You are seeing people.

Begin by naming the reality without fanfare:

> *"Someone in this room today is under a juniper tree."*

Pause. Do not add "and you know who you are." Do not qualify it. Do not soften it. Say it and let it do its work.

Then — building gradually, still slowly:

> *"You may have just come off your Mount Carmel. The hardest week. The biggest effort. The thing you gave everything to.*
>
> *And the response was not what you expected.*
>
> *And you are done.*
>
> *Not tired. Done."*

**The last line — "not tired. Done." — must be said with the distinction fully honored.** Tired is a problem with duration. Done is a problem with capacity. The congregation has felt both, and they know they are different. Honor that difference.

Then, the turn toward grace — and this is where your voice can begin to carry a slight warmth, not triumph, but kindness:

> *"God sees the empty tank.*
>
> *He knows the journey was too great.*
>
> *This Sabbath is not an accident. The fact that you are in this room — on this day — God called you here.*
>
> *Not to give you another assignment.*
>
> *To feed you."*

**Pause after "to feed you."** Do not move immediately to the verse. Let the congregation feel the shift from need to provision, from empty to met.

Then, the final statement before the verse — say it as your own voice, not a quotable:

> *"The bread is already baked. The journey was too great. And God knows it."*

---

## THE CLOSING VERSE — 1 Kings 19:7 (KJV)

> *"And the angel of the LORD came again the second time, and touched him, and said, Arise and eat; because the journey is too great for thee."*

### How to Read It

**Do not announce it as the closing verse.** Let it arrive the way the angel arrived — quietly, after the silence.

- Hold your Bible or your notes, but **look up before you begin.** Read the verse to the congregation, not at the page.
- Read at approximately two-thirds of your normal speaking pace. Slower than that risks becoming performative. This pace says: every word matters; I am not in a hurry.
- **At the comma after "second time"** — brief pause.
- **At the comma after "touched him"** — brief pause. The touch is as important as the words. Let it register.
- **"Arise and eat"** — this is where a slight lift is appropriate. Not loud. Lifted. As if the bread is actually there.
- **"Because the journey is too great for thee"** — drop back down. This is tenderness, not correction. Let the last word of the verse — *thee* — land as personal. Not "you" (generic). *Thee.* God to one man. God to one person in every pew.

### After the Verse

**Do not speak for a full five to seven seconds.**

No "amen." No "isn't that beautiful." No "let us bow in prayer" — not yet.

Let the verse sit in the room like the angel sat beside Elijah.

Then — only then — close with prayer. The prayer should be brief, pastoral, and honest. You are not asking God to do something dramatic. You are thanking Him for bread that was already baked before the congregation arrived, and asking that they receive it.

---

## TONE ARC CHECK — Does the Full Arc Hold?

Review the arc from first word to last:

| Section | Tone | What It Accomplishes |
|---|---|---|
| **Intro — The Bronx / Liverpool / Mount Carmel** | Warm, personal, unhurried | Opens trust; establishes credibility through humanity, not credentials |
| **Intro — The child who loved chapter 18** | Affectionate, slightly nostalgic | Invites the congregation into their own childhood faith; disarms intellectually |
| **Intro — The college discovery of chapter 19** | Wondering, slightly reverent | Pivots from personal memory to proclamation; names what the sermon is about |
| **Chapter 18 Bridge** | Direct, weighty, slight pause | Grounds the contrast; delivers the "one bad message" line as personal truth |
| **Points 1–3 (body)** | Exegetical, pastoral, clinical precision | Works the text; earns the application through honest engagement with the Hebrew |
| **Conclusion — Return to the boy** | Quiet, closing warmth | Closes the frame; names what grew out of the intro's discovery |
| **Conclusion — Reframe of greatness** | Declaratory, still intimate | Lands the central claim: honesty, not capacity, is Elijah's greatness |
| **Conclusion — Direct address** | Most intimate register of the sermon | Sees specific people; speaks to done-ness not tired-ness |
| **Closing verse** | Gentle, unhurried, personal | Delivers the word of God as the final pastoral act, not a proof text |
| **Silence + Prayer** | Receptive, restful | Lets the sermon become a Sabbath moment |

**Does the arc hold?** Ask yourself these three questions after your first full rehearsal:

1. **Does the personal warmth at the opening still feel present by the conclusion?** The congregation should feel that the same person who told them about Liverpool, New York is the same person now praying with them. If the conclusion feels like it belongs to a different preacher — a more formal, more distant one — you have shifted registers too hard somewhere in the body. Find the seam and soften it.

2. **Does the pastoral direct address feel earned, or announced?** The moment you say *"Someone in this room today is under a juniper tree"* should land like the recognition of something the congregation already suspected was coming — not a shift into appeal mode. If it feels like you are switching from teacher to evangelist, you have not done enough pastoral work in Points 2 and 3 to prepare the room for it. (Days 3–5 addressed this — trust the work you've done.)

3. **Is the closing verse the destination or the decoration?** The verse should feel like the reason you told the whole story. Not a supporting text. Not a capstone. The destination. If it feels decorative, spend time in rehearsal reading just the verse — alone, without context — until it carries its own weight again.

---

## THE BOOKEND TEST

The intro and conclusion should be able to stand entirely alone and still make sense as a pastoral unit. Here is the test:

**Read only the intro (without the body of the sermon) and ask:** Does this invite someone who is depleted to trust that what follows will feed them rather than demand more from them?

**Read only the conclusion (without the body of the sermon) and ask:** Does this speak to someone's actual condition without requiring them to have tracked every exegetical argument to receive the grace?

If both answer yes — the bookend holds. The sermon in the middle is the evidence. The bookends are the encounter.

---

## THREE POTENTIAL STUMBLE POINTS + RECOVERY

### Stumble 1: The intro moves too fast through the personal
**The risk:** You feel the pull to "get to the sermon" and abbreviate the Bronx / Liverpool / Mount Carmel sequence. The congregation receives an outline instead of a memory.
**Recovery:** If you feel yourself accelerating in rehearsal, build in a physical anchor. Before saying *"The church was called Mount Carmel,"* take one slow breath. The breath physically slows you. Do this at each pivot phrase in the intro until the pacing is in your body, not just your notes.

### Stumble 2: The conclusion's direct address becomes a generic altar call
**The risk:** The "someone in this room" moment slides into invitation language that could belong in any sermon. It loses the specificity of the juniper tree — the person who just came off their best effort, not merely someone who is tired.
**Recovery:** Before the direct address begins in rehearsal, spend fifteen seconds in silence first. Ask yourself: who in that congregation is actually done right now? Let a face or a situation come to mind — someone you know, or someone you imagine. Preach the direct address to that person. The specificity will follow you into the pulpit even if you do not name them.

### Stumble 3: The closing verse is read too quickly, as if it is familiar
**The risk:** Because you have lived in 1 Kings 19 for weeks, the verse no longer surprises you. You read it efficiently. The congregation, many of whom may be hearing it fresh, does not have time to receive it.
**Recovery:** In rehearsal, read the verse as if you are hearing it for the first time. Try to recover the moment when you first understood what *"because the journey is too great for thee"* meant — not as commentary, but as divine acknowledgment. That is the register you are looking for. If you can find it in rehearsal, you will find it in the pulpit.

| Conclusion: Return to the boy | ~1.5 min | Unhurried — this is the most emotionally intelligent beat of the conclusion |
| Conclusion: Reframe of greatness | ~1.5 min | The contrast list sets the pace; don't rush the "there" sequence |
| Conclusion: Direct address | ~2 min | The longest beat by feel, though not by clock — let it be slow |
| Closing verse | ~45 sec | Including the silence before and after |
| Prayer | ~45 sec–1 min | Brief. The sermon has already done the pastoral work. |
| **Conclusion Total** | **~6.5 min** | |

**Where to slow most:**
1. *"Not tired. Done."* — the distinction must land.
2. *"There. In the dust. Under a tree. Done."* — each "there" slower than the last.
3. The closing verse, particularly *"because the journey is too great for thee."*

**Where the temptation to rush will be highest:**
- The Liverpool pause (it can feel performative if you hold it too long, but the solution is not to cut it — it is to hold it with confidence rather than awkwardness)
- The direct address (because emotional proximity makes us speed up as a form of self-protection)
- After the closing verse (the silence will feel long; it is not; hold it)

## PREACHING NOTE — FOR THE PULPIT

The sermon's pastoral effectiveness depends on the first impression of warmth in the introduction. This has been said before and it bears repeating now, the day before the full run:

**The congregation will decide in the first ninety seconds whether they are safe with you.** Not whether you are intelligent. Not whether your exegesis is sound. Whether they are *safe.* The Bronx, Liverpool, Mount Carmel — these are your credentials for the vulnerable pastoral moments to come. Do not rush them. Do not perform them. Simply live in them for five minutes, and the congregation will follow you into the juniper tree and back out again.

The conclusion asks more of the congregation than any other section of the sermon. It asks them to receive care they may not feel they deserve. It asks them to be honest about being done. It asks them to hear the verse as addressed *to them,* not to Elijah.

That ask only lands if the introduction has already taught them that you are a person who has been there too — who was a child once who needed the Carmel stories, who grew into the silence of chapter 19, and who stands before them not as an expert on exhaustion but as a fellow traveler.

**The boy from the Bronx and the bread that was already baked are the same story. The sermon is the explanation. The bookends are the truth.**
