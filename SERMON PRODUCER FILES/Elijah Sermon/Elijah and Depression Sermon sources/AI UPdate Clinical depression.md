The narrative of 1 Kings 19 is frequently analyzed by theologians and mental health professionals as ==a biblical depiction of clinical depression and burnout==. It follows the prophet Elijah, who, immediately after a massive spiritual victory on Mount Carmel, experiences a severe emotional crash characterized by fear, isolation, and suicidal ideation. [1, 2, 3, 4]

## Symptoms of Depression in Elijah's Story

The text outlines several behaviors and emotions that align with modern clinical observations of depression:

- Suicidal Ideation: Elijah sits under a broom tree and prays that he might die, saying, "I have had enough, Lord. Take my life" (1 Kings 19:4).
- Isolation and Withdrawal: He leaves his servant in Beersheba and travels a day's journey alone into the wilderness to hide.
- Physical Exhaustion: He repeatedly collapses into sleep, a common symptom of depressive fatigue.
- Feelings of Worthlessness: He expresses deep self-doubt, claiming, "I am no better than my ancestors".
- Negative Self-Talk: He erroneously believes he is the only faithful person left, ignoring reality due to his emotional state. [2, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9]

## God’s "Prescription" for Elijah

Biblical scholars often highlight that God did not rebuke Elijah for his state; instead, He provided a multifaceted recovery process: [7, 9]

1. Physical Care First: Before addressing spiritual or mental issues, an angel provides Elijah with food, water, and rest. [7, 10]
2. Gentle Presence: God meets Elijah not in the dramatic wind, earthquake, or fire, but in a "gentle whisper" or "still small voice," emphasizing quiet comfort over spectacle. [2, 11]
3. Correction of Distorted Thinking: God gently challenges Elijah’s perception that he is alone by revealing there are 7,000 others who remain faithful. [2, 9]
4. Renewed Purpose: God gives Elijah new assignments, including anointing successors, which helps restore his sense of mission and future hope. [4, 9]
5. Human Connection: God instructs Elijah to find Elisha, providing him with a companion to share his burdens. [4, 9]
