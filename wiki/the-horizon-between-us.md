## Executive Summary
This essay argues that interpretation is never a neutral act because readers always arrive carrying history, training, wounds, and prior judgments. It uses clinical disagreements over the same CT scan as an entry point into philosophical hermeneutics, especially Gadamer's idea of the horizon. The piece's deepest burden is not skepticism about truth but humility about the located standpoint from which truth is pursued.

## Concepts
- Hermeneutics is framed as the disciplined study of how meaning is carried, received, and contested.
- The medical analogy shows that divergent readings can arise from different interpretive histories rather than from laziness or bad faith alone.
- Gadamer's horizon is presented as the movable boundary of vision created by one's position, not as a prison one can simply exit by force of will.
- The essay traces the widening of hermeneutics from Bible interpretation to general theories of understanding through Schleiermacher, Dilthey, Heidegger, and Gadamer.

## Insights
- The argument is strongest when it insists that every interpreter brings an entire life to the text, including Scripture.
- The clinical framing makes philosophical hermeneutics concrete and keeps the essay grounded in the author's physicianly way of seeing.
- This note sits beside theological exegesis work rather than inside it, making it a useful conceptual companion to close reading without replacing close reading.

## References
- Primary figures named in the essay include Hermes, Schleiermacher, Dilthey, Heidegger, and Gadamer.
- Nearby thematic territory: interpretation, humility, pre-understanding, and the fusion of horizons.
