# Speaker Prep Podcast Script

## Two Men, One Gospel

This script is designed as a speaker-facing audio companion, not as a public sermon recording. Its purpose is to help you internalize the logic, movement, and emotional pacing of the Braselton sermon before you preach it.

Take a breath before you begin.

You are not preaching this sermon as an attack on people who have failed. You are preaching it as a warning and an invitation. The whole burden of the sermon is this: Demas did not leave the work first. He left his love first. And Luke remained because Christ remained first.

That is the spine.

Keep that line in front of you.

When you open the sermon, do not start by explaining too much. Start with the scene. Paul is near the end. He is in prison. The letter is personal, final, urgent. Then he says, "Demas hath forsaken me, having loved this present world... only Luke is with me."

Let that sit.

Two men. One gospel. One age. One leaves. One stays.

That is enough to open the room.

Do not make Demas a cartoon villain. If you do that too early, the congregation will stand outside the sermon and judge him instead of entering the sermon and examining themselves. Demas was once a fellow laborer. Demas was once in the circle. Demas was once named alongside Luke.

That is why he is frightening.

He is not introduced to us as a rebel. He is introduced to us as a worker.

Your first movement is the tragedy of Demas.

Walk the three texts carefully.

Colossians 4:14.

Philemon 1:24.

Second Timothy 4:10.

Present. Trusted. Working. Then gone.

This is where your medical metaphor helps. Use it lightly. Say that some diseases announce themselves suddenly, but others progress quietly. Demas is not an acute collapse. He is a chronic condition of misplaced love. That line will land because it clarifies without overpowering the sermon.

Then make the key move: Paul does not say Demas became obviously wicked. Paul says Demas loved this present world.

That is the diagnosis.

Misplaced affection.

Disordered love.

A heart moving before the feet move.

From there, apply it to the congregation, but do it gently. Say that many believers are not being tempted by public scandal. They are being tempted by comfort, recognition, security, control, and respectable self-preservation. Good things become dangerous when they become ruling things.

Do not rush this section. Let people feel how close it is.

Then pivot to Luke.

Do not preach Luke as impressive in a worldly sense. Preach Luke as beautiful because he is steady.

He had skill.

He had education.

He had options.

He was a physician.

And yet when the room emptied, Luke stayed.

This is where you can tie Luke's life to Luke's Gospel. Luke wrote down the words of Jesus about denying self and taking up the cross daily. Then Luke lived those words when Paul was old, weak, and imprisoned.

That is a strong line:

Luke did not merely record the cost of discipleship. He embodied it.

Then widen the sermon from two men to the church at Ephesus.

This is essential.

Revelation 2 shows that a church can have labor, doctrine, endurance, and discernment, and still lose first love. That text prevents the congregation from thinking the sermon is only about whether someone left the church. It shows that spiritual drift can happen inside visible faithfulness.

Say it plainly:

You do not have to leave the church to leave your first love.

That line should stay.

Then bring in the Adventist turn.

Do not make the Sabbath section feel tacked on. It must feel like relief. The Sabbath is not there to make the sermon more denominational. It is there to answer the anxiety the sermon has already created.

Say that the Sabbath is God's built-in resistance against becoming Demas.

Pause after that.

Then explain:

The Sabbath interrupts identity through achievement. It interrupts striving. It interrupts the quiet replacement of God by good things. It recenters relationship over performance, trust over productivity, devotion over deliverables.

That is not legalism. That is rescue.

If you feel yourself sounding corrective or argumentative in the Sabbath section, slow down and soften. Offer it like water, not like a rulebook.

Then come to the appeal.

Use Revelation 2:5 if you want the structure:

Remember.

Repent.

Return.

Ask the heart-level questions:

What is competing with Christ for your deepest affection right now?

What has moved too close to the center?

If someone wrote one sentence about your spiritual life five years from now, would it sound more like Colossians 4 or 2 Timothy 4?

Do not over-explain those questions. Let them work.

In the final minute, move from warning to hope. The sermon should not leave people staring only at Demas. It should leave them hearing the possibility of Luke.

Say that Luke stayed not because the age was easy, but because Christ was worth more.

That is how you end.

Not with shame.

Not with fear alone.

But with reordered love.

As you preach, remember the emotional pacing:

Start reflective.

Move into diagnosis.

Then into contrast.

Then into church-wide searching.

Then into Sabbath-shaped hope.

Then into appeal.

And do not rush the silences.

Silence will preach some of this sermon for you.

After "Demas hath forsaken me..."

After "Only Luke is with me."

After "You do not have to leave the church to leave your first love."

After "The Sabbath is God's built-in resistance against becoming Demas."

And after the final question.

If you keep the sermon centered there, it will carry weight.

Your task is not to sound severe. Your task is to sound true, clear, and pastorally unafraid.

The sermon is really a prayer:

Lord, do not let us remain near the work while drifting from You. Reorder our loves. Make us steady. Make us stay.

That is the burden.

Preach it that way.
