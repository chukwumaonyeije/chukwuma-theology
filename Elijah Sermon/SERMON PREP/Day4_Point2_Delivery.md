# Day 4 of 7 — Point 2 Delivery Practice
## "The God Who Speaks in the Silence" (1 Kings 19:9–13)
**Target: 10–12 minutes | Today: Rehearse aloud twice. Time it. Memorize the pullquote word-for-word.**

---

## SPEAKING OUTLINE (bullet points — not for reading, for triggering)

**ENTRY MOVE** — Don't announce the point. Stay in the story.
- Forty days on the road. Horeb — the mountain of God.
- He finds a cave. He goes in.
- Then the word of the Lord comes to him.
- Not thunder. Not fire. A question.

**Sub-point A — The Question God Asks (v.9)**
- *"What are you doing here, Elijah?"*
- Do not let this land as a rebuke. It is not.
- This is a clinical intake question. God is not accusing — He is opening the chart.
- Note what God does NOT do first: He does not correct the theology.
  - Elijah says he's the only one left (he's not — there are seven thousand)
  - God knows this. God says nothing about it. Not yet.
- Why? Because lament requires language. Healing begins when you give the thing a name.
  - You cannot treat what has not been spoken.
  - God lets Elijah say the wrong thing, because saying the wrong thing out loud is still better than saying nothing.
- Pause. Let this breathe.
- *God is not in a hurry to correct what He hasn't yet heard.*

**PIVOT TO TRIAGE ILLUSTRATION** — [see below]

**Sub-point B — Three Events on the Mountain (vv.11–12)**
- God says: *"Go stand on the mountain before Me."*
- What follows is not subtle:
  - Wind — not a breeze. A wind that tears the mountains apart, shatters the rock.
  - Earthquake.
  - Fire.
- Three cosmic demonstrations. And then the text does something remarkable.
  - *"The Lord was not in the wind."*
  - *"The Lord was not in the earthquake."*
  - *"The Lord was not in the fire."*
- Three times the text says: not there. Not there. Not there.
- And then — *qol demamah daqah.*
  - **Three ways to carry this into the room:**
    1. The Hebrew is *the sound of thin silence* — almost the sound of no sound
    2. Or: *a still small voice* — the old King James, the one your grandmother memorized
    3. Or — most honest — *the sound of sheer silence* — what you hear when everything loud has finally stopped
  - All three are reaching for something language can barely hold.
  - It is not a whisper. It is what remains when the noise has nowhere left to go.
- And when Elijah hears it — he wraps his face in his mantle. He goes and stands at the mouth of the cave.
  - He did not go out during the wind. He did not go out during the earthquake. He did not go out during the fire.
  - The thin silence moved him.

**PIVOT TO QUIET FINDING ILLUSTRATION** — [see below]

**Sub-point C — Why Silence, Not Spectacle?**
- Elijah has been living in the loud for months.
  - Carmel was loud. Fire from heaven — loud.
  - The rain was loud. Running before the chariot was loud.
  - Jezebel's threat was loud. The wilderness was loud in a different way — the loudness of complete isolation.
- More loud would not reach him.
- This is the line. Say it slowly. Let the room receive it:
  - *"God made noise to show Elijah He could. Then He chose silence to show Elijah who He was."*
- **Pause. Full stop. Do not move.**
- SDA/Great Controversy thread — keep it tight:
  - The cosmic conflict between Christ and Satan is not primarily a war of force. It is a war of character.
  - Satan's method is spectacle — overwhelming impression that power is the point.
  - God's method is truth, love, persuasion. A voice that leaves a person free to choose.
  - The still small voice is not weakness. It is the signature of a God who does not need to shout to be sovereign.
- Close Point 2 by handing the congregation a question to carry:
  - *"What noise in your life right now is making it impossible to hear the thin silence?"*
- Let the question sit. Do not answer it for them.

## THE OBSTETRIC TRIAGE ILLUSTRATION — WITH TRANSITION LANGUAGE

### ENTERING THE MEDICAL STORY
> *"I want to put you in a triage bay with me for a moment."*

A woman walks through the doors at 11 p.m. She is pregnant. Something is wrong — she can feel it — but she cannot name it exactly. She says to the nurse: *"Something is wrong."* That is all she has.

Now — your first question as the physician is not an accusation. It is not a procedure. It is not even a test.

Your first question is: *"Tell me what brought you in tonight."*

Not because you lack instruments. Not because you don't have labs and ultrasound and monitors waiting. You ask because the patient's story matters. Because fear needs a place to go before diagnosis can begin. You let her speak. You let the anxiety come into the room. You let her name — imperfectly, incompletely — what she is carrying.

Only then do you begin the work of clarification.

### EXITING THE MEDICAL STORY AND LANDING IN THE TEXT
> *"God is the best attending physician in the room."*

When He comes to Elijah in that cave, He does not lead with correction. He does not open with a lecture about bad theology or misread circumstances. He asks the intake question.

*"What are you doing here?"*

He lets Elijah speak. He lets the lament come out — raw and partly wrong and real. He receives it without immediately fixing it.

And here is what I want someone to hear today: God does not require you to have your theology straight before He will listen to your pain. He requires only that you speak.

## THE QUIET FINDING ILLUSTRATION — WITH TRANSITION LANGUAGE

### ENTERING THE MEDICAL STORY
> *"Let me tell you something that took me years to learn in clinical practice."*

In medicine, we are trained to respond to dramatic findings. A crashing blood pressure. Labored breathing. The obvious, alarming thing that announces itself the moment you walk in the room.

But the most experienced clinicians learn something counterintuitive: the loudest finding is not always the most important one.

Sometimes it is a small shift in the ECG. A subtle change in two leads that, if you weren't looking carefully, you'd miss entirely. But a trained eye catches it — and that quiet finding explains everything else. It is the key that unlocks the whole picture.

The physician who gets captured by the drama alone — who only chases the thing that is shouting — misses the quiet finding. And sometimes that is the one that matters most.

### EXITING THE MEDICAL STORY AND LANDING IN THE TEXT
> *"God gave Elijah three dramatic findings. And then He gave him the quiet one."*

Wind. Earthquake. Fire. Everything that should announce God — everything that matched what Elijah had seen God do before — and the text says: not there. Not there. Not there.

And then *qol demamah daqah.* The thin silence. The quiet finding.

And it is the quiet finding that moves him. The wind did not move him from the cave. The earthquake did not move him. The fire did not move him. But the silence — he wraps his face in his mantle and walks to the mouth of the cave, because something in him knows: this is the one that explains everything else.

God is not impressed by His own spectacle. He uses the quiet to say what the noise cannot carry.

## *QOL DEMAMAH DAQAH* — THREE WAYS TO SAY IT FOR A CONGREGATION

Use all three in sequence. Let each one settle before moving to the next. Do not rush through them.

> *"The Hebrew phrase is qol demamah daqah — and translators have been reaching for it for centuries.*
>
> *Some say: 'a still small voice.' That's the King James. The one your grandmother may have memorized. Simple. Quiet.*
>
> *Others say: 'the sound of sheer silence' — which is almost a contradiction, isn't it? The sound of silence? But that's the point. It is so still, it has a sound.*
>
> *The most literal reading: 'the sound of thin silence.' Barely there. A thread. Almost nothing.*
>
> *What all three versions agree on: it is the opposite of everything that came before. God was not in the earthquake. God was in what was left when the earthquake was done."*

**Where to slow down most:**
1. *"Lament requires language."* — say it, pause, let it settle
2. The three translations of *qol demamah daqah* — one full breath between each
3. The pullquote: *"God made noise to show Elijah He could. Then He chose silence to show Elijah who He was."* — say it like it's the first time you've thought it

**Where to pause (full stop):**
- After: *"God does not require you to have your theology straight before He will listen to your pain."*
- After the pullquote — do not fill the silence immediately. That silence is the sermon illustrating itself.
- After the closing question — leave it open. Do not answer it.

## THREE POTENTIAL STUMBLE POINTS + RECOVERY

### Stumble 1: The three events (wind, earthquake, fire) become a list instead of an experience
**The risk:** You move through wind, earthquake, fire too quickly and the congregation doesn't feel the weight of what God was NOT in.
**Recovery:** Slow down on each "not there." Let each negative land before you move to the next. If you feel you've rushed it, don't back up — instead, before the *qol demamah daqah* moment, say: *"Three cosmic demonstrations. And the text says God was in none of them."* That recap does the work.

### Stumble 2: The *qol demamah daqah* section becomes a Hebrew lecture
**The risk:** You spend too long on etymology and lose the congregational moment.
**Recovery:** The three-translation format is your safeguard. Use it as written — one sentence each, no commentary between them. If you've over-explained, exit with: *"Whatever translation you choose — God was in what was left when the noise ran out."* Then move.

### Stumble 3: The pullquote lands flat because you haven't paused before it
**The risk:** You arrive at *"God made noise to show Elijah He could…"* while still moving from the SDA thread, and it doesn't hit.
**Recovery:** Before the pullquote, stop. Take a breath. Make eye contact. Say: *"I want to leave you with one sentence."* Then deliver it. The setup creates the landing space. Even if you forgot to pause earlier, that setup rescues the moment.
